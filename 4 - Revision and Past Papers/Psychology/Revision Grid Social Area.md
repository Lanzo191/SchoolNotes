## **Piliavin et al. (1969) - Subway Samaritan Study**

### **Background**
- Investigated real-world helping behaviour and challenged the lab-based bystander effect.  
- Focused on how **victim characteristics** (ill vs drunk) affect helping.

### **Methods Used**
- **Field experiment** on a New York subway train.

### **Sample**
- Around **4,450 passengers** (mixed ages, genders, ethnicities).  
- Opportunity sample.

### **Materials**
- Cane for “ill” victim.  
- Alcohol bottle & alcohol smell for “drunk” victim.  
- Stopwatch, standardised scripts.

### **Procedure**
1. Victim collapsed on the train after ~70 seconds of travel.  
2. Victim conditions: **Ill (cane)** vs **Drunk**.  
3. A model helped in some trials if bystanders did not.  
4. Observers recorded time to help, number of helpers, and behaviour.

### **Results**
- **95%** helped the ill victim.  
- **50%** helped the drunk victim.  
- **No strong diffusion of responsibility** found (train environment = hard to avoid).  
- Men helped more.

### **Conclusions**
- People help more when the victim appears **deserving** or safe.  
- Natural settings produce more helping compared to artificial lab studies.

### **Evaluation**
**Strengths**
- High ecological validity.  
- Large sample.  
- Quantitative + qualitative data.

**Weaknesses**
- Hard to control all variables.  
- Ethical issues: consent, potential distress.  
- Urban US bias.

## **Levine et al. (2001) - Cross-Cultural Helping Behaviour**

### **Background**
- Compared helping behaviour across cultures.  
- Interested in **economic prosperity**, **individualism vs collectivism**, and **simpatía cultures**.

### **Methods Used**
- **Cross-cultural field study** across 23 cities.

### **Sample**
- Over **1,000 participants** (opportunity sampling).  
- Citizens in major city centres.

### **Materials**
- Pen for “dropped pen” scenario.  
- Leg brace + magazines for “hurt leg.”  
- White cane + dark glasses for blind pedestrian.

### **Procedure**
Three standardised helping scenarios:
1. **Dropped pen**  
2. **Hurt leg** (struggling to pick up magazines)  
3. **Blind man** crossing the street

Helping rates were combined to give an overall score per city.

### **Results**
- **Highest helping**: Rio de Janeiro, San José, Lilongwe.  
- **Lowest helping**: New York, Kuala Lumpur, Amsterdam.  
- **Poorer cities helped more**.  
- **Simpatía cultures** scored significantly higher.

### **Conclusions**
- Helping behaviour varies greatly across cultures.  
- Cultural values and economic status strongly predict prosocial behaviour.

### **Evaluation**
**Strengths**
- Realistic tasks → high ecological validity.  
- Cross-cultural → generalisable.  
- Standardised measures.

**Weaknesses**
- Some cultural behaviour may be interpreted differently.  
- Full standardisation across countries is difficult.  
- No consent + deception.

## **Milgram et al. (1963/1965/1974) - Response to People in Authority (Obedience)**  
*(Included because it reflects behavioural [[Responses to people in need]]/power when moral conflict is present)*

### **Background**
- Investigated **obedience to authority**, inspired by crimes committed under orders (e.g., Holocaust).  
- Examined whether ordinary people would harm another person when instructed by an authority figure.

### **Methods Used**
- **Laboratory experiment** at Yale University.

### **Sample**
- **40 male volunteers**, aged 20–50.  
- Recruited through a newspaper ad.

### **Materials**
- Fake “electric shock generator” with labelled switches (15–450 volts).  
- Scripts for the experimenter and learner.  
- Lab setting and intercom.

### **Procedure**
1. Participant assigned the role of “teacher.”  
2. Confederate learner strapped to a chair (no real shocks).  
3. Teacher instructed to deliver shocks for wrong answers, increasing each time.  
4. Experimenter used scripted prompts (“the experiment requires you to continue”).  
5. Study measured: stoppage point, stress, protest.

### **Results**
- **65%** obeyed to the maximum 450 volts.  
- Participants showed visible distress (sweating, shaking, stuttering).  
- Many protested but continued when ordered.

### **Conclusions**
- Situational factors (authority, setting, obligation) heavily influence behaviour.  
- Ordinary people will obey harmful instructions even when a person appears in need.

### **Evaluation**
**Strengths**
- High control → reliable results.  
- Revealed powerful situational explanations for behaviour.

**Weaknesses**
- Ethical issues: deception, psychological harm, lack of informed consent.  
- Sample bias (male volunteers).  
- Low ecological [[Validity]] for real-world authority situations.
