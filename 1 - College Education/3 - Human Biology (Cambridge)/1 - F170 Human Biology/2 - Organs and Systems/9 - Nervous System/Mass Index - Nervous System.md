## 1. Neurons & Conduction (Deep Dive)

**Structure-Function Relationships:**
*   **Dendrites:** Highly branched to provide a large surface area for connecting with other neurons.
*   **Axon Diameter:** A larger diameter reduces resistance to ion flow, increasing conduction speed.
*   **Myelination:** Acts as an electrical insulator.
    *   *Distinction Point:* Ions cannot diffuse through myelin. Depolarisation only occurs at **Nodes of Ranvier**.
    *   **Saltatory Conduction:** Action potentials "jump" from node to node. This is faster and more energy-efficient (less ATP needed for Na+/K+ pump) than continuous conduction.

## 2. The Nerve Impulse (Mechanism Details)

**Resting Potential (-70 mV):**
*   **Crucial Detail:** The membrane is **more permeable to K+** than Na+ (many open K+ leak channels, few Na+ channels). K+ diffuses out faster than Na+ diffuses in.
*   **Active Transport:** The Na+/K+ pump transports **3 Na+ OUT** and **2 K+ IN**. This establishes the electrochemical gradient.

**Action Potential (+40 mV):**
*   **Positive Feedback:** When threshold (-55 mV) is reached, voltage-gated Na+ channels open. Na+ influx causes *more* channels to open, causing rapid depolarisation.
*   **Repolarisation:** Na+ channels close (inactivate). Voltage-gated K+ channels open. K+ leaves the cell down the electrical *and* chemical gradient.

**Refractory Period:**
*   **Mechanism:** Voltage-gated Na+ channels are temporarily inactivated (locked).
*   **Significance:**
    1.  **Unidirectionality:** Prevents the impulse from spreading backwards.
    2.  **Discrete Impulses:** Prevents action potentials from merging.
    3.  **Frequency Cap:** Limits the number of impulses per second.

## 3. Synaptic Transmission & Summation

**Cholinergic Synapse Steps (Precise Sequence):**
1.  **Arrival:** Action potential depolarises pre-synaptic knob.
2.  **Calcium Influx:** Voltage-gated **Ca²⁺ channels** open; Ca²⁺ diffuses in.
3.  **Exocytosis:** Ca²⁺ causes vesicles to fuse with the pre-synaptic membrane.
4.  **Diffusion:** Acetylcholine (ACh) crosses the cleft.
5.  **Binding:** ACh binds to **cholinergic receptors** on Na+ channels of the post-synaptic membrane.
6.  **Depolarisation:** Na+ channels open. Na+ influx causes excitatory post-synaptic potential (EPSP).
7.  **Breakdown:** **Acetylcholinesterase** hydrolyses ACh into Choline + Ethanoic Acid (recycled).

**Why is transmission Unidirectional?**
1.  Neurotransmitter vesicles are only in the **pre-synaptic** knob.
2.  Receptors are only on the **post-synaptic** membrane.

## 5. Exam-Style Application Questions

**Q1. Multiple Sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disease that destroys the myelin sheath. Explain why this leads to slower muscle response.**
*   *Answer:* Loss of myelin prevents saltatory conduction. The impulse must travel across the entire length of the axon membrane (continuous conduction) rather than jumping between nodes. This significantly reduces the speed of transmission to the muscles.

**Q2. Organophosphates (found in some pesticides) inhibit the enzyme Acetylcholinesterase. Predict the effect on the nervous system.**
*   *Answer:* Acetylcholine is not broken down in the synaptic cleft. It continues to bind to receptors on the post-synaptic membrane. This causes continuous Na+ influx and continuous generation of action potentials, leading to muscle spasms, tetany, or paralysis.

**Q3. Explain how "Temporal [[Summation]]" allows a weak stimulus to generate an [[Action Potential]].**
*   *Answer:* A single weak stimulus may release insufficient neurotransmitter to reach the threshold. However, if the neuron fires repeatedly in rapid succession (high frequency), the neurotransmitter builds up in the cleft faster than it is broken down, eventually opening enough Na+ channels to reach the threshold.
