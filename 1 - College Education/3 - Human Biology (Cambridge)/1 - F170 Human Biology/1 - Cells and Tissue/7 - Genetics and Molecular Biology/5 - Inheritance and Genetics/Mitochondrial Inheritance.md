**Mitochondrial Inheritance** follows a distinct non-Mendelian pattern because **[[Mitochondria]]** generally contain their own [[DNA]] (**mtDNA**) and are inherited exclusively from the mother.

## Key Features
- **Maternal Transmission**: The egg provides the cytoplasm and organelles (including [[Mitochondria]]) for the zygote. Sperm [[Mitochondria]] are usually destroyed or not part of the [[Fertilisation]] process.
- **All Offspring Affected**: If a mother has a mitochondrial mutation (homoplasmy), all her children (sons and daughters) will inherit it.
- **No Paternal Transmission**: Affected fathers do not pass the trait to their children.

## Homoplasmy vs Heteroplasmy
- **Homoplasmy**: All [[Mitochondria]] have the same genome (all normal or all mutant).
- **Heteroplasmy**: A mix of normal and mutant [[Mitochondria]]. The severity of the phenotype depends on the ratio of mutant to normal mtDNA (Threshold Effect).

## Comparison to Mendelian Inheritance

| Feature | Mitochondrial Inheritance | Mendelian (Nuclear) Inheritance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Transmission** | Maternal only | Both parents |
| **Genes Involved** | mtDNA | Nuclear DNA |
| **Segregation** | Random distribution of organelles | Precise segregation of chromosomes |
| **Effect on Sexes** | Both sexes affected equally | Can differ (e.g., X-linked) |

### Q) Why do fathers with mitochondrial diseases not pass them on to their children?
**Model Answer:**
The sperm's [[Mitochondria]] are located in the midpiece to provide energy for swimming. Upon [[Fertilisation]], usually only the sperm head ([[Nucleus]]) enters the egg. Even if sperm [[Mitochondria]] enter, they are typically marked for destruction (ubiquitination) and degraded by the egg cell. Thus, the zygote derives all its [[Mitochondria]] from the mother's egg cytoplasm.
