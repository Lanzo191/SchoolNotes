**Huntingdon's Disease** is a rare, progressive neurodegenerative genetic disorder.

## Genetics
- **Inheritance**: **Autosomal Dominant**.
- **Gene**: Mutation in the *HTT* gene on chromosome 4.
- **Mechanism**: The mutation involves a [[DNA]] segment known as a **CAG trinucleotide repeat**.
    - Normal: 10-35 repeats.
    - Huntingdon's: **40+ repeats**.
- This leads to the production of an abnormally long version of the **Huntingtin protein**, which damages nerve cells in the brain.

## Symptoms
- **Onset**: Usually appears in adulthood (30s-40s).
- **Motor**: Chorea (involuntary jerking movements), clumsiness.
- **Cognitive**: Difficulty concentrating, memory lapses.
- **Psychiatric**: Depression, mood swings.

## Key Feature
- Because it is **Dominant**, a child has a **50% chance** of inheriting the disease if one parent is affected (heterozygous).

## Genetic Anticipation
- In some genetic disorders like Huntingdon's, symptoms can become more severe and appear at an earlier age in successive generations.
- This is often due to the **expansion** of the unstable CAG repeat sequence during gamete formation (especially in spermatogenesis).

### Q) Explain how the molecular mechanism of Huntingdon's Disease leads to neuronal damage.
**Model Answer:**
The disease is caused by an expansion of **CAG trinucleotide repeats** in the *HTT* gene. This codes for a polyglutamine tract in the Huntingtin protein. The mutant protein misfolds and aggregates within neurons, particularly in the [[Basal Ganglia]] and cortex. These aggregates are toxic, disrupting cellular functions (like transport and [[Transcription]]) and leading to the progressive death of nerve cells.
