**Down Syndrome**, or **Trisomy 21**, is a genetic condition caused by the presence of an extra copy of chromosome 21.

## Cause
- **Non-Disjunction**: The failure of homologous [[Chromosomes]] or sister chromatids to separate properly during **[[Meiosis]]** (usually [[Meiosis]] I in the formation of the egg).
- This results in a gamete with 24 [[Chromosomes]] (n+1) instead of 23.
- Upon **[[Fertilisation]]**, the zygote has 47 [[Chromosomes]] (2n+1), with three copies of chromosome 21.

## Risk Factors
- **Maternal Age**: The risk of non-disjunction increases significantly with maternal age (older eggs).

## Clinical Characteristics
- **Physical**: Distinctive facial features (flattened face, almond-shaped eyes), short stature, single palmar crease.
- **Developmental**: Mild to moderate intellectual disability, delayed speech and motor skills.
- **Health Issues**: Increased risk of congenital heart defects, respiratory infections, and early-onset Alzheimer's.

## Karyotype
- A visual profile of the [[Chromosomes]] would show three [[Chromosomes]] at position 21.

## Diagnosis & Screening
- **Prenatal**: Can be detected via **[[Gene Screening]]** methods such as Amniocentesis, CVS, or NIPT.
- **Postnatal**: Confirmed via blood karyotyping of the infant.

---
### Q) Describe the process of Non-Disjunction that leads to Down Syndrome.
**Model Answer:**
Non-disjunction occurs during **[[Meiosis]]** (usually [[Meiosis]] I) when homologous [[Chromosomes]] fail to separate and move to opposite poles of the cell. In the case of Down Syndrome, the chromosome 21 pair remains attached. This results in one gamete (egg or sperm) receiving two copies of chromosome 21, while the other receives none. When this gamete with two copies is fertilised by a normal gamete (with one copy), the resulting zygote has three copies of chromosome 21 (Trisomy 21).
