**Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD)** is a severe type of muscular dystrophy that primarily affects boys. It is characterized by progressive muscle degeneration and weakness.

## Genetics
- **Inheritance**: **X-Linked Recessive**.
- **Gene**: Mutations in the **DMD gene** on the X chromosome.
- **Protein**: The gene codes for **Dystrophin**, a protein essential for maintaining the structural integrity of muscle cells.
- **Mechanism**: Lack of functional dystrophin leads to muscle fiber damage during contraction, inflammation, and replacement of muscle with fat and scar tissue.

## Symptoms
- Onset in early childhood (ages 2-3).
- **Gower's Sign**: Using hands to push on legs to stand up due to weak pelvic muscles.
- Enlarged calves (pseudohypertrophy).
- Loss of ambulation (walking ability) by adolescence.

## Carriers
- Females are usually carriers (heterozygous). They may be asymptomatic or have mild symptoms depending on X-inactivation patterns (**Lyonization**).

## Diagnosis
- **Creatine Kinase (CK)**: Excessively high levels of CK in the blood indicate muscle damage.
- **Genetic Testing**: Confirms mutations in the *DMD* gene.
- **Muscle Biopsy**: Shows lack of dystrophin protein.

---
### Q) Explain why Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy is much more common in males than females.
**Model Answer:**
DMD is an **X-linked recessive** disorder. Males (XY) have only one X chromosome, so a single copy of the mutated gene causes the disease (they are hemizygous). Females (XX) have two X [[Chromosomes]]; to be affected, they must inherit two mutated copies (one from each parent), which is extremely rare. Heterozygous females are carriers and usually possess enough functional dystrophin from their healthy X chromosome to avoid severe symptoms.
