## Overview: How Cells Control Expression
All cells in a multicellular organism contain the same **[[DNA]]** (the genome), but they perform different functions. This is achieved by **switching [[Genes]] on and off**. When a gene is "on", it is being transcribed into **[[mRNA]]** and translated into a **[[Proteins|protein]]**.

---

## 1. Switching Genes ON (Gene Activation)
To switch a gene on, the **[[DNA]]** must be accessible, and the machinery for **[[Transcription]]** must be recruited.

*   **Transcription Factors (Activators):** Proteins that bind to **Promoters** and help **[[RNA]] polymerase** attach to the **[[DNA]]**.
*   **Histone Acetylation:** The addition of acetyl groups to histones. This relaxes the chromatin structure (**Euchromatin**), making the **[[DNA]]** readable.
*   **Role of Hormones:** Lipid-soluble hormones like **[[Oestrogen and Gene Regulation|Oestrogen]]** enter the cell and act as transcription factors to trigger specific suites of genes.

## 2. Switching Genes OFF (Gene Silencing)
To switch a gene off, the **[[DNA]]** is either physically blocked or chemically hidden.

*   **Transcription Factors (Repressors):** Proteins that bind to the **[[DNA]]** and physically block **[[RNA]] polymerase** from binding.
*   **[[DNA]] Methylation:** The addition of methyl groups to cytosine bases. This is a stable "off switch" that prevents transcription factors from accessing the gene.
*   **Histone Deacetylation:** The removal of acetyl groups, causing the chromatin to condense into a tight package (**Heterochromatin**) that cannot be read.
*   **[[RNA]] Interference (RNAi):** A post-transcriptional "off switch" where small **[[RNA]]** molecules (siRNA) destroy **[[mRNA]]** before it can be translated into a protein.

---

## Summary Table: Control Mechanisms

| Level | Mechanism for "ON" | Mechanism for "OFF" |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Chromatin** | Acetylation (Relaxed) | Methylation / Deacetylation (Condensed) |
| **Transcriptional** | Activators / Hormones | Repressors |
| **Post-Transcriptional** | Stability of mRNA | RNA Interference (RNAi) |

### Q) Why is the ability to switch genes off just as important as switching them on?

**Model Answer:**
Switching genes off is critical for **cell differentiation** and homeostasis. If every gene were "on" all the time, cells would waste immense amounts of energy producing unnecessary **[[Proteins]]**. More importantly, specialisation would be impossible; a neuron needs to "switch off" muscle-specific genes to function correctly. Furthermore, failing to switch off genes that promote cell division (oncogenes) can lead to uncontrolled growth and **cancer**.
