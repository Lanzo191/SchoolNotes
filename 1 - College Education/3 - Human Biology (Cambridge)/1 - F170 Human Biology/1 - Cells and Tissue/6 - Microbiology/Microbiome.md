The **human microbiome** is the collective community of microorganisms (including bacteria, archaea, viruses, fungi, and protozoa) that live on and inside the human body. These communities vary by body site and interact with the host to influence digestion, [[Immunity]], development, metabolism, and disease susceptibility. Most diverse is the gut.

Understanding the microbiome requires knowledge of composition, function, methods of study, dysbiosis (imbalance), associated infections and diseases, diagnostics, therapeutic interventions, and emerging research directions.


## Overview and Definitions

- The **microbiota** refers to the actual organisms present at a body site; the **microbiome** often refers to their collective genomes and functional potential.

- Major microbial domains in humans are **Bacteria**, **Archaea**, **Fungi (mycobiome)**, **Viruses (virome)**, and occasional **protozoa**.

- Different body sites host distinct communities: **gut (largest), oral cavity, skin, [[Vagina]], respiratory tract, urogenital tract**.

- Microbial composition is described by **alpha diversity** (within-sample diversity) and **beta diversity** (between-sample differences).


## Composition by Body Site

- **Gut microbiome**: Dominated by bacterial phyla such as **Firmicutes**, **Bacteroidetes**, **Actinobacteria**, **Proteobacteria**, and **Verrucomicrobia**. It contains anaerobes (e.g., Bacteroides, Clostridia) and facultative anaerobes.

- **Oral microbiome**: Highly diverse; includes Streptococcus, Actinomyces, Veillonella and anaerobes in subgingival niches.

- **Skin microbiome**: Includes Staphylococcus, Cutibacterium (formerly Propionibacterium), Corynebacterium; composition differs by sebaceous, moist, and dry sites.

- **Vaginal microbiome**: Often dominated by Lactobacillus species in healthy reproductive-age women; composition shifts during menstruation, pregnancy, or infection.

- **Respiratory microbiome**: Upper airway more diverse; lower airway has lower biomass but resident organisms and transient microbes from aspiration.

- **Placental and fetal microbiome**: Controversial; most evidence supports that initial colonisation mainly occurs at birth and postnatally.


## Development and Determinants of the Microbiome

- **Early life colonisation**: Mode of birth (vaginal vs caesarean), breastfeeding vs formula, antibiotic exposure, maternal microbiota, and environment shape neonatal microbiome.

- **Diet**: Major determinant: long-term dietary patterns (high fibre vs high fat/sugar) modify community composition and metabolic outputs (e.g., SCFAs).

- **Antibiotics and drugs**: Cause rapid shifts and reduced diversity; effects can be long-lasting and promote resistance.

- **Age and physiology**: Microbiome changes across lifespan: infant → adult → elderly with altered diversity and stability.

- **Genetics and [[Immunity]]**: Host genotype, immune factors, and mucosal environment influence which microbes persist.

- **Environment and lifestyle**: Hygiene, sanitation, geography, pets, and social contacts all alter exposure and composition.


## Key Functions of the Microbiome

- **Digestive and metabolic roles**: Fermentation of dietary fibres to produce **short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)**: mainly **acetate, propionate, and butyrate**: which provide energy to colonocytes and modulate host metabolism.

- **Vitamin synthesis**: Microbes synthesise vitamins such as **vitamin K** and certain B vitamins.

- **Bile acid metabolism**: Microbial deconjugation and modification of bile acids affects lipid absorption and signalling via nuclear receptors (FXR, TGR5).

- **Colonisation resistance**: Resident microbes prevent pathogen overgrowth by competition for nutrients and niche, and by producing antimicrobial compounds.

- **Immune system development and modulation**: Microbiota shape mucosal and systemic [[Immunity]] (e.g., induction of regulatory T cells, balancing Th1/Th2/Th17 responses).

- **Barrier integrity**: Microbes and their metabolites support mucous layer thickness and tight junction function.

- **Neural interactions**: The **gut–brain axis** links microbial metabolites (SCFAs, tryptophan metabolites), vagal signalling, and immune mediators to influence behaviour, mood, and neurodevelopment.


## Important Microbial Metabolites and Mechanisms

- **Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)**: Butyrate fuels colonocytes and promotes anti-inflammatory regulatory T cells; propionate and acetate influence gluconeogenesis, lipogenesis, and appetite signalling.

- **Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)**: Component of Gram-negative bacteria; high systemic LPS levels (metabolic endotoxemia) can promote inflammation and [[Insulin]] resistance.

- **Trimethylamine (TMA) → TMAO**: Microbial metabolism of dietary choline and carnitine produces TMA, converted in [[Liver|Liver]] to TMAO associated with cardiovascular disease risk.

- **Bacterial peptides and cell-wall components**: Stimulate pattern-recognition receptors (e.g., TLRs) on immune cells to shape responses.

- **Tryptophan metabolites**: Indole derivatives modulate mucosal [[Immunity]] and the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) pathways with effects on inflammation and barrier function.


## Methods to Study the Microbiome

- **Culture-based methods**: Useful for isolating viable organisms but miss many anaerobes and unculturable taxa.

- **16S [[rRNA]] gene sequencing**: Targets bacterial taxonomic marker [[Genes]] to profile composition; limited resolution to species and no direct functional data.

- **Metagenomics (shotgun sequencing)**: Sequences whole-community [[DNA]] to infer taxonomic profiles and predict [[Genes]]/metabolic pathways.

- **Metatranscriptomics**: Sequences community [[RNA]] to see which [[Genes]] are actively expressed.

- **Metabolomics**: Measures small-molecule metabolites (SCFAs, bile acids, TMAO) to infer functional output.

- **Proteomics and culturomics**: Complementary methods to detect [[Proteins]] and culture previously uncultured organisms.

- **Limitations**: Contamination, sample storage, sequencing depth, bioinformatic pipelines, and difficulty distinguishing correlation from causation.


## Dysbiosis: Definition and Causes

- **Dysbiosis** is an imbalance in microbial composition and function associated with disease.

- Causes include antibiotics, poor diet, infection, inflammation, stress, hygiene changes, and host genetics.

- Dysbiosis is characterised by **reduced diversity**, loss of beneficial microbes, and overgrowth of opportunistic/pathogenic taxa.


## Infections and Diseases Associated with the Microbiome

### Gastrointestinal Diseases

- **Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI)**: Often follows broad-spectrum antibiotics that disrupt gut microbiota; produces toxins causing severe diarrhoea, pseudomembranous colitis, and can be recurrent.

- **Helicobacter pylori**: Colonises the [[Stomach]], can cause gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, and increases risk for gastric cancer. It is not typically part of the gut microbiome but is a key pathobiont.

- **Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD: Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis)**: Linked to dysbiosis with reduced Firmicutes (butyrate producers) and increased Proteobacteria; immune dysregulation and genetic susceptibility are central.

- **Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)**: Altered microbiota composition correlates with symptoms; mechanisms include altered fermentation, gas production, and immune activation.

- **Colorectal cancer**: Certain bacteria (e.g., Fusobacterium nucleatum, enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis) are associated with tumourigenesis via inflammation, genotoxins, and immune modulation.

- **Malabsorption and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO)**: Excess bacteria in [[Small Intestine]] cause bloating, diarrhea, and nutrient deficiencies.


### Metabolic and Cardiovascular Diseases

- **Obesity and metabolic syndrome**: Associations with higher Firmicutes:Bacteroidetes ratio in some studies, altered SCFA profiles, and microbial contributions to energy harvest and inflammation.

- **Type 2 [[Diabetes]] (T2D)**: Dysbiosis correlates with decreased butyrate producers, increased pro-inflammatory taxa, and altered bile acid metabolism.

- **Cardiovascular disease**: Microbial-derived TMAO and inflammatory mediators can contribute to atherosclerosis risk.


### Immune and Autoimmune Conditions

- **Allergies and asthma**: Early-life microbial exposures shape Th1/Th2 balance; reduced microbial diversity and altered gut microbiota are linked to increased allergy and asthma risk.

- **Autoimmune diseases**: Dysbiosis has been associated with rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, and type 1 [[Diabetes]], possibly via molecular mimicry, barrier dysfunction, and immune modulation.


### Neurological and Psychiatric Conditions

- **Gut–brain axis**: Alterations in gut microbiota are linked to depression, anxiety, autism spectrum disorders, and neurodegenerative diseases through immune, neural, and metabolic pathways. Evidence includes animal models and human [[Correlations]], but causation is complex.


### Skin and Oral Diseases

- **Atopic dermatitis (eczema)**: Reduced skin microbial diversity and Staphylococcus ureus overgrowth correlate with severity.

- **Acne**: Changes in Cutibacterium acnes strains and inflammatory responses contribute to pathology.

- **Periodontitis and dental caries**: Dysbiosis in oral biofilms with pathogenic bacteria (Porphyromonas gingivalis, Streptococcus mutans) leads to gum disease and tooth decay.


### Vaginal and Urogenital Conditions

- **Bacterial vaginosis (BV)**: Loss of Lactobacillus dominance and overgrowth of anaerobes (Gardnerella, Atopobium) cause malodorous discharge and increased STI risk.

- **Yeast infections (Candida spp.)**: Overgrowth linked to dysbiosis, antibiotics, and immune suppression.


### Opportunistic Infections and Sepsis

- **Opportunistic pathogens** (e.g., Candida, Enterococcus, Pseudomonas) can cause severe disease when host [[Immunity]] is compromised.

- **Sepsis** can be triggered by translocation of gut microbes or systemic infection, with microbiome alterations implicated in susceptibility and outcomes.


### Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR)

- The microbiome is a reservoir for **antimicrobial resistance [[Genes]] (the resistome)**; antibiotic use selects for resistance and horizontal gene transfer among microbes amplifies spread.


## Diagnostics and Biomarkers

- **Stool sequencing and metabolite profiling** offer non-invasive windows into gut microbiome state.

- **Specific markers**: Reduced butyrate producers, increased inflammatory taxa, and certain metabolite signatures (TMAO, SCFA ratios) can act as biomarkers.

- **Clinical tests**: PCR for pathogens (C. difficile, H. pylori), stool calprotectin for intestinal inflammation, breath tests for SIBO or carbohydrate malabsorption.

- **Limitations**: High inter-individual variability and confounding by diet, medications, and sample collection design.


## Therapeutic Interventions and Modulation

- **Antibiotics**: Treat infections but disrupt microbiome and promote resistance; targeted narrow-spectrum agents preferred when possible.

- **Probiotics**: Live microorganisms (e.g., Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Saccharomyces boulardii) that can restore balance in certain conditions (e.g., reduce antibiotic-associated diarrhoea, prevent CDI recurrence in some contexts). Efficacy is strain-specific.

- **Prebiotics**: Non-digestible fibres (inulin, fructooligosaccharides) that stimulate growth of beneficial microbes and increase SCFA production.

- **Synbiotics**: Combination of prebiotics and probiotics designed to synergise.

- **Postbiotics**: Microbial metabolites or inactivated microbial components used for therapeutic effects (emerging area).

- **Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT)**: Transfer of stool from healthy donors to patients to restore a healthy microbiome: highly effective for recurrent C. difficile infection and being trialled for IBD, metabolic disease, and other indications.

- **Dietary interventions**: High-fibre, plant-rich diets increase diversity and SCFA production; ketogenic or high-fat diets shift composition differently.

- **Bacteriophage therapy**: Viruses that target specific bacteria offer species-specific modulation and are under investigation for AMR and dysbiosis treatment.

- **Microbiome-based drugs**: Engineered bacteria or metabolites aimed at modulating host pathways are in development.


## Mechanisms of Disease Causation

- **Loss of beneficial microbes** reduces SCFA production and barrier support, increasing inflammation.

- **Overgrowth of pathobionts** produces toxins, genotoxins, or metabolites that damage tissue or promote carcinogenesis.

- **Immune dysregulation**: Altered microbial signals skew T-cell responses, promoting chronic inflammation or autoimmunity.

- **Metabolic disruption**: Altered bile acid profiles, TMAO production, and energy harvest contribute to metabolic disease.


## Prevention and Public Health Considerations

- **Antibiotic stewardship** to minimise unnecessary exposure and preserve microbial diversity.

- **Dietary public health**: Promote fibre-rich diets for a healthy microbiome.

- **Infection control**: Hygiene balanced with exposure to diverse microbes (the “old friends” hypothesis) to reduce allergy/autoimmune risk.

- **Vaccination and maternal health**: Maternal microbiome and health influence infant colonisation; vaccination prevents infections that would otherwise require antibiotics.


## Research, Limitations, and Future Directions

- **Causality vs correlation**: Many human studies are associative; rigorous longitudinal and interventional trials are required to establish causation.

- **Personalised microbiome medicine**: Aim to tailor probiotics, diet, and therapeutics to individual microbiomes.

- **[[Standardisation|Standardisation]] needs**: Protocols for sampling, storage, sequencing, and analysis must be standardised to improve reproducibility.

- **Microbiome engineering**: Synthetic biology approaches aim to create designer microbes for therapy.

- **Ethical and safety concerns**: Donor selection for FMT, long-term consequences of altering communities, and horizontal gene transfer risks must be considered.


## Practical Exam-Focused Summary Points

- The gut microbiome produces **SCFAs** (butyrate, propionate, acetate) that maintain colonic health and modulate [[Immunity]].

- **Antibiotics** can precipitate **C. difficile** infection by disrupting colonisation resistance.

- **Dysbiosis** is implicated in IBD, IBS, obesity, T2D, allergies, and may influence mental health via the gut–brain axis.

- **FMT** is an effective therapy for recurrent C. difficile and is under research for other indications.

- Diagnostic approaches include **16S [[rRNA]] sequencing** for composition and **metabolomics** for functional readouts; both have limitations.


## Key Terms to Remember

- Microbiome, microbiota, dysbiosis, colonisation resistance, SCFAs, metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, resistome, FMT, probiotics, prebiotics, pathobiont.


## Suggested Practical/Experimental Notes (for coursework)

- When designing microbiome experiments, control for **diet, antibiotics, sampling time, and storage**.

- Use **negative controls** to detect contamination, especially for low-biomass sites (e.g., [[Lungs]]).

- Combine **taxonomic (16S)** and **functional (metagenomics/metabolomics)** methods to link composition to function.
