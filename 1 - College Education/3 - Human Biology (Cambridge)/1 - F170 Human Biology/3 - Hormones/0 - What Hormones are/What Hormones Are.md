## What ACTUALLY Are Hormones?

Hormones are **molecules** - actual physical chemical substances floating in your bloodstream. They're not signals or energy or electrical impulses - they're real molecules made of atoms, just like [[Glucose]] or water. They're produced by endocrine glands and travel dissolved in blood plasma.

Think of it like this: your [[Pancreas]] literally makes [[Insulin]] molecules (chains of [[Amino Acids]] folded into a specific 3D shape), releases thousands of them into your blood, and they physically travel around your body until they bump into and bind to [[Insulin]] receptors on [[Cell Membranes]].

## What Are They Made Of?

### 1. Peptide/Protein Hormones

**Made of: Chains of [[Amino Acids]]**

- **Short peptides** (3-50 [[Amino Acids]]): e.g., ADH, oxytocin
- **Longer [[Proteins]]** (51+ [[Amino Acids]]): e.g., [[Insulin]], growth hormone, FSH, LH

**Structure**: [[Amino Acids]] joined by peptide bonds, folded into specific 3D shapes

- [[Insulin]] has 51 [[Amino Acids]] in two chains linked by disulfide bridges
- The sequence and folding determines what receptor it fits into

**Properties**:

- Hydrophilic (water-soluble) because of polar amino acid side chains
- Cannot pass through lipid bilayer membranes
- Stored in vesicles inside endocrine cells until needed

### 2. Steroid Hormones

**Made of: Lipids derived from cholesterol**

Examples: Oestrogen, testosterone, progesterone, [[Cortisol]], aldosterone

**Structure**: Four carbon rings fused together (derived from cholesterol molecule)

- Different side groups attached to the rings make different steroids
- Testosterone and oestrogen are almost identical molecules - just tiny differences in their structure

**Properties**:

- Lipid-soluble (hydrophobic) - can dissolve through [[Cell Membranes]]
- Not stored - synthesized on demand from cholesterol
- Travel in blood attached to carrier [[Proteins]] (because lipids don't dissolve well in watery blood)

### 3. Amino Acid Derivatives

**Made of: Modified single [[Amino Acids]]**

Examples:

- **[[Adrenaline]] & [[Noradrenaline]]** - derived from tyrosine
- **Thyroxine (T4)** - derived from tyrosine with iodine atoms attached

**Structure**: Single [[Amino Acids]] with chemical groups added

- [[Adrenaline]] is literally tyrosine with extra -OH and -CH₃ groups attached

**Properties**: Varies - some are hydrophilic ([[Adrenaline]]), some lipophilic (thyroxine)

## The Physical Reality

When your [[Pancreas]] detects high blood [[Glucose]]:

1. β cells literally **synthesize** [[Insulin]] molecules ([[Ribosomes]] make the amino acid chain)
2. These molecules are **packaged** into vesicles
3. Vesicles fuse with the cell membrane and **dump** thousands of [[Insulin]] molecules into blood [[Capillaries]]
4. These molecules **physically drift** through your bloodstream, bouncing off blood vessel walls
5. When an [[Insulin]] molecule randomly **collides** with an [[Insulin]] receptor (complementary shape), it **binds** - like a 3D jigsaw piece
6. This binding **physically changes** the receptor protein's shape
7. The shape change triggers chemical reactions inside that cell

It's all physical chemistry - molecules bumping into each other, binding based on complementary shapes, causing mechanical changes in protein structures.

## How Hormones Are Made

**Peptide hormones**:

- Made by [[Ribosomes]] on rough ER (just like any protein)
- Modified in [[Golgi Apparatus]]
- Stored in secretory vesicles
- Released by [[Exocytosis]] when triggered

**Steroid hormones**:

- Made in smooth ER from cholesterol
- Enzymes add/remove chemical groups to cholesterol
- Diffuse straight out of the cell (can pass through membranes)
- Made "on demand" - not stored

**Mode of Action**

- Secreted directly into the bloodstream (endocrine secretion)
- Transported in blood plasma to reach target cells
- Act at low concentrations
- Bind to specific receptors on/in target cells
- Can have widespread or localised effects depending on receptor distribution

## How Hormones Work

### Target Cell Specificity

- Only cells with **complementary receptors** respond to a particular hormone
- Receptor-hormone binding is like a lock and key mechanism
- This explains why hormones can travel throughout the body but only affect specific tissues

### Two Main Mechanisms

**1. Peptide Hormones (e.g., [[Insulin]], ADH)**

- Cannot pass through cell membrane (hydrophilic)
- Bind to receptors on cell surface membrane
- Trigger a cascade of reactions inside the cell via **second messengers** (e.g., cyclic AMP)
- Cause rapid but temporary effects

**2. Steroid Hormones (e.g., oestrogen, testosterone)**

- Can pass through cell membrane (lipid-soluble)
- Bind to receptors inside the cell (cytoplasm or [[Nucleus]])
- Hormone-receptor complex acts as a [[Transcription]] factor
- Affects gene expression directly
- Cause slower but longer-lasting effects

## Hormones vs Nervous System

|Feature|Endocrine System (Hormones)|Nervous System|
|---|---|---|
|Transmission|Chemical via blood|Electrical impulses|
|Speed|Slower|Very rapid|
|Duration|Long-lasting effects|Short-lived effects|
|Target|Widespread (can affect multiple organs)|Specific/localised|
|Response type|Usually gradual|Immediate|

## Examples You Need to Know (OCR)

### Blood Glucose Regulation

- **Insulin** - lowers blood glucose (secreted by β cells in pancreas)
- **Glucagon** - raises blood glucose (secreted by α cells in pancreas)

### Water Potential Regulation (Osmoregulation)

- **ADH (Antidiuretic Hormone)** - increases water reabsorption in kidneys
- Secreted by posterior pituitary gland
- Acts on collecting ducts in nephrons

### Reproductive Hormones

- **Oestrogen** - female sex hormone, regulates menstrual cycle
- **Testosterone** - male sex hormone
- **LH and FSH** - control gamete production and hormone release

### Stress Response

- **Adrenaline** - "fight or flight" hormone
- Increases heart rate, blood glucose, breathing rate

## Negative Feedback

Most hormones are regulated by **negative feedback loops**:

1. A change in condition is detected
2. Hormone is secreted to correct the change
3. Once condition returns to normal, hormone secretion is reduced
4. This maintains homeostasis

**Example**: Blood glucose regulation

- High blood glucose → insulin released → glucose decreases → insulin secretion stops
- Low blood glucose → glucagon released → glucose increases → glucagon secretion stops

## Key Terms

- **Endocrine gland** - ductless gland that secretes hormones directly into blood
- **Target cell/organ** - cells with specific receptors for a particular hormone
- **Receptor** - protein molecule with complementary shape to specific hormone
- **Second messenger** - molecule inside cell triggered by hormone binding (e.g., cAMP)
- **Homeostasis** - maintenance of stable internal conditions
