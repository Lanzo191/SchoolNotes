**Proteins** are large, complex organic molecules made primarily of **carbon (C), hydrogen (H), oxygen (O), nitrogen (N)**, and in some cases sulfur (S). They are polymers of **[[Amino Acids]]** linked by **peptide bonds**, forming polypeptides that fold into functional three-dimensional structures. Proteins are essential for virtually all biological processes in the human body and represent the most functionally diverse class of biological macromolecules.

## Fundamentals of Protein Structure

### What Proteins Look Like - The Four Structural Levels

#### Primary Structure

The linear sequence of [[Amino Acids]] in a polypeptide chain, determined by the nucleotide sequence of DNA. Think of it as a string of beads, where each bead is an amino acid connected by peptide bonds.

- Written N-terminus to C-terminus (e.g., Met-Ala-Gly-Leu-Trp...)
- The order of [[Amino Acids]] dictates all higher-level structures and the protein's final function
- Even a single amino acid substitution can drastically affect function (e.g., sickle-cell disease: glutamate → valine at position 6 of beta-globin)

#### Secondary Structure

Local, regular folding patterns of the polypeptide backbone stabilised by **hydrogen bonds** between backbone atoms (C=O and N-H groups).

**Alpha helix (α-helix):**

- Right-handed spiral/coil structure resembling a spring or telephone cord
- Each turn contains ~3.6 amino acids
- Hydrogen bonds form between C=O of one amino acid and N-H of amino acid four positions ahead
- Very stable and common in fibrous proteins like keratin (hair, nails)
- Provides strength and rigidity

**Beta-pleated sheet (β-sheet):**

- Extended polypeptide chains lying side-by-side forming a sheet
- Hydrogen bonds form between adjacent strands
- Can be **parallel** (chains run in same direction) or **antiparallel** (opposite directions - more stable)
- Creates flat, pleated surface like folded paper
- Found in silk fibroin, giving silk its strength

**Turns and loops:** Connect helices and sheets, allowing the chain to reverse direction and fold back on itself

#### Tertiary Structure

The complete three-dimensional shape of a single polypeptide chain - how the entire molecule folds up in space.

**Stabilised by interactions between R groups (side chains):**

1. **Disulfide bridges (S-S bonds):** Covalent bonds between two cysteine residues; strongest interaction; permanent unless chemically broken
2. **Ionic bonds (salt bridges):** Electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged R groups (e.g., lysine+ and aspartate-)
3. **Hydrogen bonds:** Between polar R groups (e.g., serine and asparagine)
4. **Hydrophobic interactions:** Nonpolar R groups cluster together in the protein's core, away from water; main driving force of folding
5. **Van der Waals forces:** Weak attractions between atoms in close proximity

**The hydrophobic effect:** Hydrophobic amino acids (leucine, valine, phenylalanine) bury themselves inside the protein, while hydrophilic amino acids (serine, lysine, aspartate) face outward toward the aqueous environment. This is the primary force driving protein folding.

**Result:** Creates the protein's **active site** (for enzymes) or **binding site** (for receptors and transport proteins), determining its specific function.

#### Quaternary Structure

The arrangement of multiple polypeptide chains (subunits) into a single functional protein complex.

- Held together by the same non-covalent interactions as tertiary structure (plus sometimes disulfide bonds)
- **Example - Haemoglobin:** Four subunits (two alpha chains + two beta chains) work together to transport oxygen cooperatively
- **Example - Collagen:** Three polypeptide chains wind together in a triple helix, providing enormous tensile strength
- **Example - Antibodies:** Two heavy chains + two light chains linked by disulfide bonds

Not all proteins have quaternary structure - only those made of multiple polypeptide chains.

## How Amino Acids Connect: Peptide Bond Chemistry

### The Peptide Bond

- Forms through a **condensation reaction (dehydration synthesis)** between two [[Amino Acids]]
- The **carboxyl group (-COOH)** of one amino acid reacts with the **amino group (-NH₂)** of another
- Releases a **water molecule (H₂O)**
- Creates a **C-N covalent bond** called a peptide bond

**Structure:** -CO-NH- (the carbonyl carbon bonded to the nitrogen)

### Properties of Peptide Bonds

- **Rigid and planar:** Has partial double-bond character due to resonance, preventing rotation
- **Trans configuration:** R groups on opposite sides (more stable)
- **Strong:** Requires enzymes (proteases) to break during digestion
- The chain can only rotate around bonds connected to the alpha carbon, which determines how the protein can fold

### Polypeptide Directionality

Every polypeptide has two distinct ends:

- **N-terminus:** Free amino group (-NH₂) at the start
- **C-terminus:** Free carboxyl group (-COOH) at the end
- Sequences are always written N → C (left to right)

## The 20 Standard Amino Acids

Each amino acid has a central **alpha carbon (Cα)** bonded to four groups:

1. **Amino group (-NH₂)**
2. **Carboxyl group (-COOH)**
3. **Hydrogen atom (-H)**
4. **R group (side chain)** - determines the amino acid's unique properties

### Classification by R Group Properties

**Nonpolar/Hydrophobic:** Glycine, Alanine, Valine, Leucine, Isoleucine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Tryptophan, Proline

- Avoid water; found in protein interiors
- Provide structural stability through hydrophobic interactions

**Polar/Uncharged:** Serine, Threonine, Cysteine, Tyrosine, Asparagine, Glutamine

- Can form hydrogen bonds with water or other polar molecules
- Serine, threonine, and tyrosine can be phosphorylated (regulation)
- Cysteine contains sulfur and can form disulfide bridges

**Positively Charged (Basic):** Lysine, Arginine, Histidine

- Attract negative charges
- Important in binding DNA/RNA and catalysis

**Negatively Charged (Acidic):** Aspartate, Glutamate

- Attract positive charges
- Important in enzyme active sites and calcium binding

### Essential vs Non-Essential Amino Acids

**Essential** (must be obtained from diet): Histidine, Isoleucine, Leucine, Lysine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Valine

**Non-essential** (can be synthesised by the body): Alanine, Asparagine, Aspartate, Cysteine, Glutamate, Glutamine, Glycine, Proline, Serine, Tyrosine

**Conditionally essential:** Arginine (required during growth and stress)

## Protein Folding and Stability

### How Proteins Fold

- Folding begins during translation (as the chain emerges from the ribosome)
- The primary structure determines the final 3D shape
- **Chaperone proteins** (e.g., heat shock proteins) assist folding by:
    - Preventing aggregation
    - Providing isolated environment
    - Unfolding misfolded proteins for another attempt
- Folding is driven toward the **lowest free energy state** (most thermodynamically stable)

### Denaturation - Loss of Structure

Proteins can lose their 3D structure (unfold) without breaking peptide bonds.

**Causes:**

- **Heat:** Disrupts hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions
- **Extreme pH:** Changes ionisation of R groups, breaking ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds
- **Heavy metals:** Bind to sulfur in cysteine, disrupting structure
- **Organic solvents:** Disrupt hydrophobic interactions
- **Mechanical agitation:** Physical force (e.g., whipping egg whites)

**Result:** Loss of biological activity (enzymes stop working, antibodies can't bind antigens)

**Reversibility:** Some proteins can **renature** (refold) if conditions return to normal, but aggregation often makes denaturation irreversible.

## Functional Roles of Proteins in the Body

### 1. Structural Proteins

Provide mechanical support, strength, and shape to cells and tissues.

**Collagen:**

- Most abundant protein in mammals (~30% of total body protein)
- Three polypeptide chains twisted into a triple helix
- High in glycine and proline
- Provides tensile strength to skin, bones, tendons, ligaments, blood vessels
- Different types (I, II, III, IV) in different tissues

**Keratin:**

- Alpha-helical protein rich in cysteine
- Extensive disulfide bridges provide strength and rigidity
- Found in hair, nails, outer layer of skin
- **Hard keratin** (more S-S bonds) in hair/nails vs **soft keratin** in skin

**Elastin:**

- Provides elasticity and recoil
- Found in lungs, arteries, skin, ligaments
- Allows tissues to stretch and return to original shape

**Actin and Myosin:**

- Contractile proteins in muscle tissue
- Actin forms thin filaments, myosin forms thick filaments
- Slide past each other during muscle contraction (sliding filament theory)

### 2. Enzymes (Biological Catalysts)

Speed up biochemical reactions by lowering activation energy without being consumed.

**Key features:**

- **Highly specific:** Each enzyme catalyses one type of reaction
- **Active site:** 3D pocket where substrate binds
- **Enzyme-substrate complex:** Temporary binding stabilises transition state
- **Unchanged after reaction:** Can be used repeatedly

**Factors affecting enzyme activity:**

- **Temperature:** Higher temperature increases reaction rate until denaturation occurs (optimal ~37°C for human enzymes)
- **pH:** Each enzyme has optimal pH (pepsin: pH 2, trypsin: pH 8); extremes cause denaturation
- **Substrate concentration:** Reaction rate increases until all enzyme active sites are saturated (Vmax)
- **Cofactors:** Non-protein helpers (metal ions like Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺)
- **Coenzymes:** Organic cofactors (vitamins like NAD⁺, FAD, coenzyme A)
- **Inhibitors:** Molecules that reduce enzyme activity (competitive or non-competitive)

**Examples:** Amylase (starch digestion), DNA polymerase (DNA replication), ATP synthase (energy production), catalase (breaks down hydrogen peroxide)

### 3. Transport Proteins

Carry molecules across membranes or through body fluids.

**Haemoglobin:**

- Transports oxygen from lungs to tissues and CO₂ in reverse
- Four subunits (2 alpha + 2 beta chains), each with a haem group containing iron (Fe²⁺)
- **Cooperative binding:** Oxygen binding to one subunit increases affinity of others (sigmoidal binding curve)
- Affected by pH (Bohr effect), temperature, and 2,3-BPG
- One molecule can carry four oxygen molecules

**Myoglobin:**

- Single polypeptide with one haem group
- Stores oxygen in muscle tissue
- Higher oxygen affinity than haemoglobin

**Membrane transport proteins:**

- **Channel proteins:** Form pores for passive diffusion (e.g., aquaporins for water, ion channels)
- **Carrier proteins:** Bind and transport specific molecules (e.g., glucose transporters GLUT1-4)
- **Pumps:** Use ATP to move molecules against concentration gradient (e.g., sodium-potassium pump)

**Blood plasma proteins:**

- **Albumin:** Maintains osmotic pressure; transports fatty acids, hormones, drugs
- **Transferrin:** Transports iron
- **Lipoproteins:** Transport lipids (LDL, HDL, VLDL)

### 4. Hormonal Signalling

Protein hormones regulate metabolism, growth, and reproduction.

**[[Insulin]]:**

- Produced by pancreatic beta cells
- Lowers blood glucose by promoting glucose uptake into cells
- Stimulates glycogen synthesis in liver and muscle
- Two polypeptide chains (A and B) linked by disulfide bonds

**Glucagon:**

- Produced by pancreatic alpha cells
- Raises blood glucose by promoting glycogen breakdown
- Antagonistic to insulin

**Growth Hormone (GH):**

- Stimulates growth, cell reproduction, regeneration
- Released by anterior pituitary gland

**Others:** TSH, FSH, LH, ACTH, prolactin

**Mechanism:** Protein hormones bind to cell surface receptors (cannot cross membrane) → trigger signal transduction cascades via second messengers (cAMP, Ca²⁺, IP₃) → cellular response

### 5. Immune Defence (Antibodies)

Immunoglobulins produced by B lymphocytes to neutralise pathogens.

**Structure:**

- Y-shaped quaternary structure
- Two identical heavy chains + two identical light chains
- Linked by disulfide bonds
- **Variable regions** (tips of Y) bind specific antigens with high specificity
- **Constant regions** determine antibody class and effector functions

**Five classes:**

- **IgG:** Most abundant in blood; crosses placenta; long-term immunity
- **IgM:** First antibody produced in immune response; pentamer structure
- **IgA:** Found in mucous membranes, saliva, tears, breast milk
- **IgE:** Involved in allergic reactions; binds to mast cells and basophils
- **IgD:** Functions as B cell receptor

**Functions:**

- **Neutralisation:** Block pathogen binding to host cells
- **Opsonisation:** Mark pathogens for phagocytosis
- **Complement activation:** Trigger membrane attack complex
- **Agglutination:** Clump pathogens together for easier elimination

### 6. Storage Proteins

Store amino acids or bind molecules for later use.

**Ferritin:**

- Stores iron in liver, spleen, bone marrow
- Spherical protein shell that can hold up to 4,500 iron atoms
- Prevents iron toxicity while keeping it bioavailable
- Blood ferritin levels indicate iron stores

**Others:** Casein (milk protein for infant nutrition), Ovalbumin (egg white)

### 7. Contractile and Motor Proteins

Enable movement at cellular and organismal levels.

**Actin and Myosin:**

- Interact in muscle contraction
- Myosin heads walk along actin filaments using ATP energy
- Produces force and shortening (sliding filament mechanism)

**Tubulin:**

- Forms microtubules for cell structure and chromosome movement
- Component of cilia and flagella

**Dynein and Kinesin:**

- Motor proteins that walk along microtubules
- Transport organelles and vesicles within cells

### 8. Receptor Proteins

Detect chemical signals and initiate cellular responses.

**Types:**

- **G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs):** Bind hormones, neurotransmitters; activate intracellular signalling
- **Receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs):** Bind growth factors; trigger phosphorylation cascades
- **Ion channel receptors:** Neurotransmitter receptors that open/close ion channels
- **Nuclear receptors:** Bind steroid hormones; directly regulate gene transcription

### 9. Other Important Proteins

**Blood clotting:** Fibrinogen, thrombin, clotting factors **Protection:** Mucins (mucus), interferons (antiviral) **Regulation:** Transcription factors, histones (DNA packaging)

## Protein Digestion and Absorption

### Stomach

- **Denaturation:** Hydrochloric acid (pH 1.5-2.0) unfolds proteins by disrupting hydrogen bonds and ionic interactions
- **Pepsin:** Chief cells secrete pepsinogen → activated to pepsin by HCl
- Pepsin breaks peptide bonds, especially between hydrophobic amino acids
- Produces polypeptides and peptides

### Small Intestine

- **Pancreatic proteases** secreted as inactive zymogens (prevent self-digestion):
    
    - **Trypsinogen → Trypsin** (activated by enterokinase from intestinal wall)
    - **Chymotrypsinogen → Chymotrypsin** (activated by trypsin)
    - **Procarboxypeptidase → Carboxypeptidase** (activated by trypsin)
- **Trypsin:** Cuts after lysine and arginine (basic amino acids)
    
- **Chymotrypsin:** Cuts after large hydrophobic amino acids (phenylalanine, tryptophan, tyrosine)
    
- **Carboxypeptidase:** Removes amino acids from C-terminus
    

Result: Dipeptides, tripeptides, and free [[Amino Acids]]

### Final Absorption

- **Brush border enzymes** on [[Microvilli]] of intestinal cells:
    
    - **Aminopeptidases:** Remove amino acids from N-terminus
    - **Dipeptidases:** Break dipeptides into single amino acids
- **Absorption:** [[Amino Acids]] absorbed via **[[Active Transport]]** using Na⁺-dependent cotransporters
    
- Different transporters for different amino acid types (neutral, acidic, basic)
    
- Di- and tripeptides can be absorbed and broken down inside intestinal cells
    
- [[Amino Acids]] enter blood and travel via hepatic portal vein to [[Liver]]

## Protein Metabolism

### Amino Acid Pool

- Free [[Amino Acids]] in blood and cells available for protein synthesis
- Continuously replenished from dietary protein and protein turnover
- Body proteins constantly broken down and resynthesised
- Half-life varies: enzymes (hours), structural proteins (days to months)

### Protein Synthesis

- Occurs on ribosomes via translation
- Requires mRNA template, tRNA with amino acids, ATP/GTP energy
- **Post-translational modifications** enhance function:
    - **Phosphorylation:** Regulates enzyme activity
    - **Glycosylation:** Adds sugars for cell recognition
    - **Ubiquitination:** Marks proteins for degradation
    - **Proteolytic cleavage:** Activates inactive precursors (e.g., pepsinogen → pepsin)

### Amino Acid Catabolism (Breakdown)

- Excess [[Amino Acids]] cannot be stored (unlike glucose or fat)
- **Deamination:** Amino group removed in liver, producing ammonia (NH₃) + carbon skeleton
- **Transamination:** Amino group transferred to alpha-ketoglutarate → glutamate
- **Urea cycle:** Toxic ammonia converted to urea for excretion by kidneys
- **Carbon skeletons:**
    - **Glucogenic amino acids:** Enter gluconeogenesis → glucose
    - **Ketogenic amino acids:** Form ketone bodies or acetyl-CoA
    - Can enter citric acid cycle → ATP production

### Proteins as Energy Source

- Provide approximately **17 kJ/g (4 kcal/g)** when oxidised
- Not a preferred energy source (carbohydrates and fats used first)
- Used significantly during:
    - Prolonged fasting or starvation
    - Very low-carbohydrate diets
    - Intense prolonged exercise when glycogen depleted
    - Disease states (diabetes, cancer cachexia)

## Dietary Requirements and Protein Quality

### Recommended Intake

- **Adults:** 0.8 g protein/kg body weight per day
- **Higher needs:** Growth (children, adolescents), pregnancy, lactation, illness, intense exercise, elderly
- **Athletes:** 1.2-2.0 g/kg per day depending on sport

### Protein Quality

**Complete proteins:** Contain all essential amino acids in adequate amounts

- Animal sources: meat, fish, eggs, dairy
- Plant sources: soy, quinoa

**Incomplete proteins:** Lack one or more essential amino acids

- Most plant sources: grains, legumes, nuts, vegetables
- **Complementary proteins:** Combining incomplete sources provides all essential amino acids (e.g., rice + beans)

**Biological Value (BV):** Proportion of absorbed protein retained for growth/maintenance (egg = 100, beef = 80, beans = 49)

## Clinical and Pathological Relevance

### Protein Deficiency

**Kwashiorkor:**

- Inadequate protein despite sufficient calories
- Symptoms: oedema (low albumin), muscle wasting, fatty liver, impaired immunity, stunted growth
- Common in children after weaning to low-protein diet

**Marasmus:**

- Total calorie and protein deficiency
- Severe wasting, loss of fat and muscle, no oedema

### Proteinuria

- Protein in urine indicates [[Kidney]] dysfunction
- Normal kidneys filter but reabsorb proteins
- Causes: glomerular damage (diabetes), tubular damage, overflow (multiple myeloma)
- **Albuminuria** is early marker of kidney disease

### Genetic Mutations Affecting Proteins

**Sickle-cell disease:**

- Single amino acid substitution (glutamate → valine at position 6 of beta-globin)
- Abnormal haemoglobin polymerises under low oxygen
- Red blood cells become sickle-shaped
- Causes vascular occlusion, pain, organ damage

**Cystic fibrosis:**

- Mutation in CFTR chloride channel protein
- Thick mucus in lungs and digestive tract

**Phenylketonuria (PKU):**

- Deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase enzyme
- Phenylalanine accumulates → brain damage if untreated
- Requires low-phenylalanine diet from birth

### Enzyme Denaturation

- Extreme pH or temperatures alter tertiary structure
- Active site shape changes → enzyme loses function
- Metabolism impaired
- Example: High fever can denature body enzymes

### Excessive Protein Intake

- **Kidney strain:** Increased nitrogen waste requires more filtration
- **Dehydration:** Protein metabolism produces urea requiring water for elimination
- **Liver stress:** Increased urea cycle workload
- May increase calcium excretion (bone health concern - debated)

### Protein Misfolding Diseases

**Prion diseases:** Misfolded prion proteins cause normal prions to misfold → brain damage (Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, mad cow disease)

**Alzheimer's disease:** Beta-amyloid plaques and tau protein tangles

**Parkinson's disease:** Alpha-synuclein protein aggregates

## Summary

Proteins are the most functionally diverse biological macromolecules, essential for **structural support, enzymatic catalysis, transport, hormonal regulation, immune defence, storage, movement, and energy**. Their structure follows a hierarchy from **primary sequence** (amino acid order) through **secondary** (helices/sheets), **tertiary** (3D folding), to **quaternary** (multi-subunit complexes). Protein function is determined by **amino acid sequence, 3D shape, and environmental factors** (pH, temperature, cofactors). [[Amino Acids]] connect via rigid **peptide bonds** formed through condensation reactions, and proteins fold driven by the **hydrophobic effect** and stabilised by various interactions (disulfide bonds, ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions). Any disruption to structure through mutation, denaturation, or misfolding can have severe physiological consequences, leading to enzyme dysfunction, transport defects, immune disorders, and disease. Understanding protein chemistry, structure, and metabolism is fundamental to comprehending human biology and medicine.
