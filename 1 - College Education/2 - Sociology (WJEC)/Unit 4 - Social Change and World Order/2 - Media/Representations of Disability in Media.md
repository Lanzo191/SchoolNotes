Disabled people are significantly under-represented in the media. When they do appear, it is usually to fit a specific "narrative" rather than as a normal person.

## The Medical Model vs The Social Model
*   **Medical Model:** The media usually focuses on the disability as a "problem" or "tragedy" within the individual that needs curing.
*   **Social Model:** Rarely shown. This would focus on how society disables people through barriers (e.g., lack of ramps), rather than the impairment itself.

## Barnes (1992): Stereotypes of Disability
Barnes identified several recurring "tropes":

1.  **The Pitiable / Victim:** Encourages audiences to feel sorry for them (e.g., Telethons like *Children in Need*). Reinforces viewing disabled people as dependent.
2.  **The Super-Cripple:** Someone who "overcomes" their disability in a heroic way (e.g., Paralympians). While positive, it suggests that disabled people must be "extraordinary" to be accepted.
3.  **The Villain / Monster:** Physical deformity is used to signify evil (e.g., Bond villains with scars, Captain Hook).
4.  **The Burden:** Portrayed as a drain on their family or society.
5.  **Atmosphere:** Used to create a spooky or uneasy atmosphere.

## Roper (2003): The Telethon Critique
Argues that charity telethons (like *Telethon* or *Children in Need*) rely on "cute" children to generate guilt and pity. This keeps disabled people in the position of passive recipients of charity, rather than empowered citizens fighting for rights.

---

## Changing Representations
*   **Paralympics (2012 onwards):** Significant shift towards viewing disabled athletes as powerful and elite ("Superhumans").
*   **Broadcasting Acts:** Requires channels to improve diversity. We now see more disabled presenters (e.g., on *CBeebies* or *The Last Leg*) where the disability is incidental, not the focus.
