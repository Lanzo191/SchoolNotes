## Overview
Sociologists are interested in how the **Media** doesn't just report on youth culture and deviance, but actively **constructs** it. Through the use of labels, stereotypes, and sensationalism, the media can shape how young people see themselves and how society reacts to them.

---

## 1. Moral Panics and Folk Devils
As seen in the work of **[[Moral Panics and Folk Devils|Stanley Cohen]]**, the media often targets specific youth subcultures (e.g., Mods, Rockers, Punks, or "Hoodies").
*   **Labeling:** The media labels a group as a threat to societal values.
*   **Folk Devils:** The group is simplified and demonised into a "Folk Devil."
*   **Social Control:** This triggers a reaction from "Moral Entrepreneurs" (politicians/police) to "crack down" on the group.

## 2. Deviancy Amplification
This is the process where the media’s attempt to control deviance actually produces **more** of it.
*   **Notoriety:** By reporting on a subculture, the media makes it famous, attracting more young people who want to rebel.
*   **Self-Fulfilling Prophecy:** Young people may begin to act out the deviant "role" the media has assigned them.
*   **Polarisation:** Clashes between groups (like the Mods and Rockers) were often made worse because the media told each group where and when to meet for a "fight."

## 3. Subcultural Capital (Sarah Thornton, 1995)
Thornton updated the idea of moral panics by studying **Club Culture** and Raves in the 1990s.
*   **Media as a Tool:** She found that youth subcultures actually **liked** being reported on negatively in the "mainstream" media.
*   **Subcultural Capital:** Being labelled a "Folk Devil" gave the youth "street cred" or **subcultural capital** within their own group.
*   **The Niche Media:** Subcultures use their own media (zines, blogs, pirate radio) to resist mainstream labels.

## 5. Criticisms of Moral Panic Theory
While the theory is widely used, it has faced several significant criticisms:

*   **Frequency and "Jaded" Audiences (McRobbie & Thornton, 1995):** They argue that moral panics are now so frequent that they have lost their power to shock. The public has become "jaded" and less likely to react with genuine panic.
*   **The Rise of New Media:** In the age of social media and 24-hour news, it is much harder for a single newspaper or TV station to create a unified national "panic." There are now many competing voices and "counter-narratives."
*   **Realist Critique (Left Realism):** Left Realists argue that by calling a social reaction a "panic," sociologists imply that the fear is irrational or disproportionate. They argue that issues like **knife crime** or **gang violence** are real and serious threats to working-class communities, not just "media constructions."
*   **Difficulty of Measurement:** It is very difficult to prove that a reaction is "disproportionate." What one person sees as a panic, another might see as a reasonable concern for public safety.

### Q) Outline and explain the role of "Folk Devils" in the media. (10 marks)

**Model Answer:**
In sociology, a **Folk Devil** is a person or group that is portrayed by the media as an outsider and a major threat to the values and order of society. The term was coined by **Stanley Cohen** in his study of the **Mods and Rockers**.

The media creates Folk Devils by using **sensationalist language** and stereotypes to simplify complex social issues. For example, by blaming "hoodies" for a rise in crime, the media provides a clear target for public anger. This process is essential for starting a **Moral Panic**, as it puts pressure on the government and police to take action against the group. However, as **Sarah Thornton** notes, being labelled a Folk Devil can sometimes backfire, as it provides young people with **subcultural capital** and makes the deviant lifestyle seem more attractive to potential new members.
