## McRobbie and Garber (1975) - Girls and Subcultures

### 1. Theoretical Background
*   **Feminism:** A perspective that highlights how traditional sociology has ignored or marginalized the experiences of women and girls (**"Malestream" sociology**).
*   **Bedroom Culture:** The private subcultural space developed by girls, characterized by activities like listening to music, reading magazines, and discussing boys.
*   **Social Control:** The way society (and specifically parents) regulates the behavior of individuals. Girls historically faced stricter control than boys.

### 2. Aim
To investigate why girls were less visible in studies of **youth subcultures** and to explore the unique ways that girls experience and resist social norms.

### 3. Methodology
*   **Method:** Theoretical analysis and **ethnographic observation**.
*   **Perspective:** **Feminist**.
*   **Focus:** The social life of teenage girls and their exclusion from "street-based" subcultures.

### 4. Key Findings
*   **Invisible Resistance:** Girls were less visible in studies like the Mods or Teddy Boys because they were often excluded from street culture or were only seen as "girlfriends" of the male members.
*   **Bedroom Culture:** Because of stricter **social control** at home, girls developed their own subculture in the privacy of their bedrooms. This involved experimenting with makeup, reading *Jackie* magazine, and following pop idols.
*   **Consumerism:** Girls' subcultures were more heavily influenced by consumer products (fashion and magazines), which reinforced domestic gender roles.

### 5. Conclusion
*   Youth subcultures are not just male-dominated; girls have their own distinct forms of subcultural activity that take place in **private spaces**.
*   The invisibility of girls in previous studies was due to **researcher bias** and the different levels of social freedom granted to boys and girls.

### 6. Detailed Evaluation & Improvements

| Feature | Evaluation & Details |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Research Method** | **Ethnographic Observation**: Provided a new and necessary focus on the internal lives of girls. |
| **Perspective** | **Feminist**: Challenged the "Malestream" bias in sociology and broadened the definition of a subculture. |
| **Sample** | **Teenage Girls**: Focused specifically on working-class girls in the 1970s. |
| **Sampling Method** | **N/A**: Theoretical and ethnographic focus. |
| **Data Type** | **Qualitative Data**: Rich descriptions of "Bedroom Culture" and magazine content analysis. |
| **Ethics** | **Good**: Highlighted the inequalities and restrictions faced by young women, promoting social awareness. |
| **Weakness** | **Historical Bias**: The original "Bedroom Culture" is based on the 1970s; modern girls have more freedom to occupy public spaces. |
| **Reliability Improvements** | **Consistency**: The concept has been updated to include **"Virtual Bedroom Culture,"** showing its continued relevance in the digital age. |
| **Validity Improvements** | **Researcher Reflexivity**: By questioning their own role as researchers, they identified the gender bias in earlier studies. |

### Q1) What is meant by "Bedroom Culture" in this study? (2 marks)
**Model Answer:** **Bedroom Culture** refers to the private subcultural activities of teenage girls, such as reading magazines and listening to music, which occurred in the home due to the stricter **social control** and lack of freedom compared to boys.

---

### Q2) Explain why McRobbie and Garber criticized previous studies of youth culture (3 marks)
**Model Answer:** They criticized previous studies for being **"Malestream"**, meaning they were conducted by men and focused almost entirely on male, street-based subcultures. They argued that girls were either ignored entirely or only mentioned as passive accessories to boys, failing to recognize girls' **active participation** in their own subcultures.

---

### Q3) Discuss how the concept of Bedroom Culture applies to the modern digital age (3 marks)
**Model Answer:** In the modern age, the concept has evolved into **"Virtual Bedroom Culture."** While girls still use their bedrooms as a private space, they now use social media and smartphones to participate in global digital communities and subcultures from their rooms, showing that McRobbie and Garber's focus on **private resistance** is still highly relevant.
