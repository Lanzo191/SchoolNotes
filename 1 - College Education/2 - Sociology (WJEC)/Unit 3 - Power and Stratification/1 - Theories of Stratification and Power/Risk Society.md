## Concept of the Risk Society
- Formulated by German sociologist **Ulrich Beck** (1992) and expanded by **Anthony Giddens**.
- A risk society is a modern social order organized around the management, perception, and distribution of risks created by modernization and technological progress.
- Classical industrial society focused on the distribution of 'goods' (wealth and resources); late modern society focuses equally on the distribution of 'bads' (manufactured risks and hazards).

## Manufactured Risks vs Natural Risks

| Risk Category | Definition | Key Characteristics | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Natural Risks** | Hazards arising from natural forces outside human creation | Predictable within historical bounds; localized in scope | Earthquakes, floods, seasonal droughts |
| **Manufactured Risks** | Insecurities generated directly by human scientific, industrial, and technological developments | Global in scope; irreversible; difficult to calculate or insure against | Nuclear disasters (Chernobyl), chemical pollution, climate change, AI disruption |

## Core Characteristics of the Risk Society
- **Preoccupation with Future Hazards**: Public discourse and government policy become dominated by preventative safety, threat assessment, and damage control.
- **Global Scope**: Manufactured risks cross national boundaries; toxic contamination, atmospheric pollution, and financial crises cannot be confined to their country of origin.
- **Reflexivity**: Society becomes self-conscious and self-critical, constantly monitoring its own processes and adjusting policies in response to recognized hazards.
- **Unequal Risk Exposure**: While risks affect populations globally, the ability to avoid or insulate oneself from risk remains stratified by wealth, geographical location, and social resources.
- **Erosion of Institutional Trust**: Catastrophic technological failures undermine public faith in scientific experts, corporate assurances, and state regulatory bodies.
