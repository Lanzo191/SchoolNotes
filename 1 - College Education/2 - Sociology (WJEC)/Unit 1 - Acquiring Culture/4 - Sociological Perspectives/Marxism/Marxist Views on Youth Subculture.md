## Overview
**[[Marxism|Marxists]]** suggest that society is dominated by the rich capitalist **Bourgeoisie** because they control the norms and values of society:a concept **Antonio Gramsci** called **Hegemony**. Marxists argue that the poor working class use youth subcultures as a way to **resist** this domination.

---

## The CCCS (Birmingham School)
The **Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies (CCCS)** at the University of Birmingham was the leading school for Marxist subcultural theory in the 1970s. They argued that subcultures were a form of **"Resistance through Rituals"** (Hall and Jefferson, 1976).

### 1. Phil Cohen (1972) : Skinheads
*   **Study:** Argued that **Skinhead** culture was a symbolic reaction to the decline of traditional working-class communities in the East End of London.
*   **Resistance:** By wearing manual worker clothes (boots, jeans, braces), they were "re-creating" a traditional working-class identity that was being lost to urban redevelopment and the rise of middle-class values.

### 2. Hall and Jefferson (1976) : Teddy Boys
*   Teddy Boys emerged in the 1950s. Their style (Edwardian-style jackets) was a way of **symbolically** reclaiming a status that was denied to them by their low-paid jobs and lack of opportunities.

### 3. Dick Hebdige (1979) : Bricolage and Punk
*   **Concept:** Hebdige used the term **"Bricolage"** to describe how subcultures (like Punks) take everyday objects (like safety pins, bin bags, or school uniforms) and give them new, rebellious meanings.
*   **Resistance:** This was a way to subvert the dominant culture. However, Hebdige also identified **incorporation** (or **commodification**) : where capitalism "steals" the subculture's style and sells it back to the masses as fashion, thus stripping it of its rebellious meaning.

## Paul Willis (1977) : "Learning to Labour"

### Methodology
Willis used **participant observation** and group interviews to study a group of working-class boys in the Midlands, whom he called **"the lads."**

### Key Findings
*   **The Counter-School Subculture:** "The lads" rejected the school's values and authority, creating a subculture based on "having a laugh" and disrupting lessons.
*   **Manual vs Mental Labour:** They identified strongly with **manual labour** (working with hands) and mocked academic work as "feminine" or "soft."
*   **Self-Damnation:** While their subculture was a form of **resistance**, it ultimately led to their "self-damnation." By rejecting school, they ensured they would only be qualified for low-paid, manual jobs, thus helping to maintain the **Capitalist** system they were trying to resist.

### Q) Evaluate the Marxist view that youth subcultures are a form of resistance. (15 marks)

**Model Answer:**
Marxists, particularly those associated with the **CCCS**, argue that youth subcultures are a way for the working class to resist ruling-class **Hegemony**. Rather than seeing subcultures as simple rebellion, they see them as **symbolic resistance** against economic exploitation.

For example, **Dick Hebdige** argued that **Punks** used **bricolage** to subvert capitalist symbols, while **Phil Cohen** saw **Skinheads** as trying to reclaim a lost working-class identity. These groups use style and behavior to "win space" from a society that ignores them.

However, **Paul Willis** provides a more complex view. In his study **"Learning to Labour"**, he found that while "the lads" resisted school authority, this resistance was ultimately **counter-productive**. It led to their **"self-damnation"** as they ended up in the very manual jobs the capitalist system required. Furthermore, critics argue that Marxists read too much "politics" into youth groups, ignoring that many young people join subcultures simply for fun, fashion, or music, rather than to resist capitalism.

---

**Revision Tip:** When discussing subcultures, use the term **"Symbolic Resistance."** This is the idea that they aren't trying to start a physical revolution, but are using their *style* and *clothing* to say "no" to the system.
