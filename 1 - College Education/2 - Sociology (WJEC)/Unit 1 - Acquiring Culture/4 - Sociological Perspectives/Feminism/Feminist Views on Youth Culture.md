## Overview
**Feminist** sociologists criticise other theories (like **[[Functionalism]]** and **[[Marxism]]**) for being **"Malestream"**:meaning they focus almost exclusively on male subcultures and ignore the experiences of girls. They argue that girls' roles in subcultures have been marginalised or rendered invisible in traditional research.

---

## Key Studies

### 1. McRobbie and Garber (1976) - Bedroom Culture
*   **Overview:** They argued that girls were absent from street-based subcultures because they were subject to stricter **[[Social Control]]** by their parents compared to boys.
*   **Concept:** Consequently, girls formed their own distinct subculture centred around the home, termed **"Bedroom Culture"**.
*   **Characteristics:** Focused on listening to music, reading magazines, and discussing relationships. It provided a safe space away from male harassment and parental supervision.

### 2. Smart (1976) - Safety and Control
*   Smart argued that parents exercise stricter control over their daughters' leisure time due to **fears for their safety**, which limits their participation in visible, "risky" street subcultures like the **Mods or Rockers**.

### 3. Lincoln (2004) - Bedroom Culture Revisited
*   Lincoln found that **Bedroom Culture** has evolved. With the internet and social media, the bedroom is no longer a place of isolation but a hub of global communication. It is now a **"digital bedroom"** culture.

### 4. Blackman (1995) - New Wave Girls
*   Studied a group of working-class girls who formed a high-profile, academic, and **anti-sexist** subculture. They used their subculture to resist both the school's patriarchal regime and traditional teacher expectations.

### Q) Evaluate the Feminist claim that youth culture research is "malestream". (15 marks)

**Model Answer:**
Feminists argue that traditional sociology is **"malestream"** because it focuses on male-dominated street subcultures (like skinheads or punks) while ignoring the experiences of girls. Early researchers, such as those from the **CCCS**, often assumed that girls were either absent or merely "accessories" to male groups.

**McRobbie and Garber** challenged this by identifying **"Bedroom Culture"**. They argued that girls weren't "less" active, but that their subculture was private due to stricter **[[Social Control]]** from parents. This "bedroom culture" provided a space for girls to experiment with identity and resist patriarchal expectations in private.

However, modern research like **Blackman's** "New Wave Girls" shows that girls are increasingly active in visible, public subcultures. Furthermore, with the rise of the internet, **Lincoln** argues that the boundaries between private and public have blurred. While the "malestream" criticism was highly valid in the 1970s, modern sociology is much more inclusive of gender diversity, though Feminists would argue that patriarchal pressures still shape how girls participate.

---

**Revision Tip:** When discussing McRobbie and Garber, always use the term **"invisible"**. Girls weren't missing from society; they were just invisible to male researchers who only looked at the streets.
