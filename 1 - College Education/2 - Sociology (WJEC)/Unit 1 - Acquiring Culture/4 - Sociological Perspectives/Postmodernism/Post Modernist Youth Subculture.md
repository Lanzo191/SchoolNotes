## Overview -
**Postmodernists** argue that old sociological theories ([[Marxism]], [[Functionalism]]) are outdated. They believe that in a postmodern society, youth subcultures are no longer based on fixed identities like **Social Class** or **Gender**. 

- Instead, youth culture is about **fluidity**, **choice**, and **consumption**.
- People can pick and choose elements from different subcultures.

## Key Concepts -

### 1. Polhemus (1994) - The "Supermarket of Style"
*   Polhemus argued that youth today have a vast choice of styles, music, and identities to choose from.
*   They "browse" through different subcultures and mix elements together (e.g., wearing punk boots with a hip-hop jacket).
*   Identity is no longer a "label" but a choice of fashion and lifestyle.

### 2. Maffesoli (1996) - Neo-Tribes
*   Instead of rigid subcultures, Maffesoli suggested the term **"Neo-Tribes."**
*   These are loose, temporary groupings of young people who share a similar taste in music or fashion.
*   They are not based on class resistance; they are based on shared consumption and "having a laugh."
*   Membership is fluid:you can belong to multiple "tribes" at once or move between them easily.

### 3. Bennett (1999) - Clubcultures
*   Studied the "clubbing" scene in Newcastle.
*   Found that people at clubs weren't resisting anything; they were just there for the music and the atmosphere.
*   Support Maffesoli’s idea that youth identities are fragmented and temporary.

## Evaluation -
### Strengths
*   Recognises that modern youth culture is much more diverse and less rigid than in the 1960s/70s.
*   Highlights the importance of the **media** and **globalisation** in providing choices.

### Weaknesses
*   **Marxists** argue that Postmodernists ignore the fact that social class *still* affects how much money young people have to spend in the "supermarket of style."
*   **Feminists** argue that gender still plays a role in how young people are treated in certain subcultures (e.g., "[[Cock Rock]]").
*   Ignores that some subcultures are still very rigid and committed (e.g., certain religious or political subcultures).
