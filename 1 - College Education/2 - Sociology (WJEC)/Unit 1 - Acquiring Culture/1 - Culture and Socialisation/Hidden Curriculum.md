## Overview -
The **Hidden Curriculum** refers to the unspoken, unwritten, and unofficial lessons, values, and perspectives that students learn in school. Unlike the formal curriculum (subjects like Maths or English), these lessons are not part of the official timetable but are taught through the school's structure and social environment.

## Key Examples -
*   **Hierarchy:** Learning to respect the authority of teachers and staff, preparing students for the hierarchy of the workplace.
*   **Competition:** Through sports and grading, students learn that society is competitive and that there are "winners" and "losers."
*   **Punctuality:** Learning that being on time is essential (e.g., bells, registers).
*   **Gender Roles:** Sometimes reinforced through teacher expectations or certain subjects being seen as "for boys" or "for girls."

## Sociological Perspectives -

### 1. Functionalism (Consensus View)
*   Functionalists see the hidden curriculum as **positive**.
*   It provides **[[Secondary Socialisation]]**, teaching children the universalistic values of wider society.
*   **Parsons** argues it helps children transition from the "particularistic" standards of the family to the "universalistic" standards of the adult world.
*   It creates **Social Solidarity**.

### 2. Marxism (Conflict View)
*   Marxists see the hidden curriculum as **negative** and a tool of [[Social Control]].
*   **Bowles and Gintis (The Correspondence Principle):** They argue the hidden curriculum "corresponds" to the needs of the capitalist workplace.
*   It produces a **subservient workforce** that is used to following orders without question.
*   It justifies inequality by teaching students that their failure is their own fault (meritocracy is a myth).

### 3. Feminism
*   Feminists argue the hidden curriculum can reinforce **[[Patriarchy]]**.
*   It may encourage girls to be passive and boys to be dominant through teacher attention and gendered play.
