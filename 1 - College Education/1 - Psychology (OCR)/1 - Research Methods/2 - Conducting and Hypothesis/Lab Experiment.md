## Overview -
A **laboratory experiment** is conducted in a highly controlled, often artificial environment. The researcher deliberately manipulates the [[Independent Variable]] (IV) to see the effect on the [[Dependent Variable]] (DV), while keeping all other variables constant.

## Key Features -
*   **Manipulation:** The researcher directly changes the IV.
*   **Control:** High control over **[[Extraneous Variables]]** to prevent them from becoming [[Confounding Variables]].
*   **[[Standardisation]]:** Every participant has exactly the same experience (same instructions, same room, etc.).
*   **Allocation:** Participants are often **randomly allocated** to different conditions to reduce bias.

## Strengths -
*   **Internal [[Validity]]:** High control means we can be reasonably sure that the change in the DV was caused by the IV (**[[Cause and Effect]]**).
*   **Reliability:** Easy to replicate exactly due to [[Standardisation]].
*   **Precision:** Allows for the use of complex equipment (e.g., brain scanners) that wouldn't work in the field.

## Weaknesses -
*   **Low Ecological [[Validity]]:** The artificial setting and task may cause participants to act unnaturally.
*   **[[Demand Characteristics]]:** Participants are more likely to guess the aim of the study in a lab.
*   **Investigator Effects:** The researcher's presence may influence participant behaviour.

## Core Study Example -
*   **Milgram (1963):** Used a controlled lab setting at Yale to measure obedience. The setting was crucial for creating the "Legitimacy of Authority."
