Internal reliability refers to the **consistency of results within a test or measurement itself**.  
It assesses whether **different parts of the same test** produce **similar results**, indicating that all items are measuring the same underlying concept.

### Key Idea -  
If a test has good internal reliability, all its questions or components should **correlate well with one another**, suggesting they measure the **same construct** consistently.

### Methods of Assessing Internal Reliability -  

- **Split-Half Method -**  
  The test is split into two halves (e.g., odd vs. even questions).  
  The two halves are then compared for consistency.  
  A strong correlation between both halves indicates **high internal reliability**.  

- **Cronbach’s Alpha -**  
  A statistical test used to assess **average correlation among all items** in a scale or questionnaire.  
  Values range from 0 to 1:  
  - 0.7 and above = generally acceptable reliability  
  - 0.8–0.9 = good reliability  
  - Above 0.9 = may indicate redundancy (items too similar)  

- **Item-Total Correlation -**  
  Each item is correlated with the total score from all other items.  
  Low [[Correlations]] suggest that an item might not fit with the rest of the scale.  

### Improving Internal Reliability -  
- Reword unclear or ambiguous items.  
- Remove or replace items that don’t correlate with the rest.  
- Standardise instructions and procedures.  
- Pilot the test and analyse consistency statistically before main use.

### Examples -  
- A questionnaire measuring **anxiety** should produce consistent responses across items about physiological, cognitive, and behavioural symptoms.  
- If half the questions give very different results from the others, internal reliability is low.

### Importance -  
Internal reliability ensures that a test is **coherent and dependable**, allowing researchers to make accurate conclusions about behaviour or psychological traits.  
Poor internal reliability makes data less valid and interpretations unreliable.
