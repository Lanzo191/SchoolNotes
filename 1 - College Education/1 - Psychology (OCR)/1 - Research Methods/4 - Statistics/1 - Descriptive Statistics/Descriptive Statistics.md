**Descriptive Statistics** are used to summarise and describe data so that patterns or trends can be identified. They do **not** allow researchers to prove cause and effect (that's what **[[Inferential Statistics]]** are for).

---

## 1. Measures of Central Tendency (Averages)

| Measure | **How to do it** | **Strengths** | **Weaknesses** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Mean** | Add all scores and divide by total number. | Uses every single piece of data. | Easily distorted by **outliers** (extreme scores). |
| **Median** | The middle score in a ranked list. | Not affected by outliers. | Less sensitive (doesn't use every score). |
| **Mode** | The most frequent score. | Good for **Nominal** (category) data. | Not useful if there is more than one mode. |

---

## 2. Measures of Dispersion (Spread)

| Measure | **How to do it** | **Strengths** | **Weaknesses** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Range** | Highest score minus lowest score. | Very simple and quick to calculate. | Only uses two scores; ignores the rest. |
| **Standard Deviation** | Shows how much scores "stray" from the mean. | A precise measure of how consistent results are. | More complex and harder to understand. |

## Summary Tip
- **Low Standard Deviation** = Results are consistent (most people got similar scores).
- **High Standard Deviation** = Results are varied (people got very different scores).
