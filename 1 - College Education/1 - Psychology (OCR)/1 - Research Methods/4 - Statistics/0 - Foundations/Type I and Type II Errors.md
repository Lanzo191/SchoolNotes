When psychologists perform a statistical test, they can sometimes make a mistake in their conclusion. These are known as **Type I** and **Type II** errors.

---

## 1. Type I Error (The "False Positive")
A Type I error occurs when the researcher **rejects the null hypothesis** even though it was actually **true**.
- **In Plain English**: You think you found something interesting, but it was actually just a fluke.
- **Why it happens**: Usually because the significance level was set too high (e.g., $p=0.10$).
- **Memory Trick**: Type **I** is like saying "**I** found something!" (but you're wrong).

---

## 2. Type II Error (The "False Negative")
A Type II error occurs when the researcher **accepts the null hypothesis** even though it was actually **false**.
- **In Plain English**: You think there's nothing interesting going on, but you actually missed a real effect!
- **Why it happens**: Usually because the significance level was set too low (e.g., $p=0.01$) or the sample size was too small.
- **Memory Trick**: Type **II** is like saying "**II** missed it!" (Two "I"s for "missed it").

---

## Significance and Errors

The risk of these errors is directly linked to your **$p$ value**.

| Significance Level | **Risk of Type I (False Positive)** | **Risk of Type II (False Negative)** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$p=0.10$** (Too lenient) | **High** | **Low** |
| **$p=0.05$** (Standard) | **Low** | **Low** |
| **$p=0.01$** (Too strict) | **Very Low** | **High** |

---

## OCR Exam Question Practice
> **Q: A researcher used a $p=0.01$ level of significance. Which type of error are they MOST likely to make?**
> **A: Type II.** (Because they are being so strict that they might miss a real effect).
