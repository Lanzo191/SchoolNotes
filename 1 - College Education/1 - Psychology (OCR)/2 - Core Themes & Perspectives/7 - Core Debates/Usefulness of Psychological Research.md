## Definition of Usefulness
- Psychological research is considered useful when it makes valid, positive contributions to theoretical understanding or produces practical applications outside the research setting.
- Usefulness is relative and depends directly on methodological validity: findings must be valid (internal and ecological) and reliable to provide a safe foundation for real-world application.

## Criteria for Evaluating Usefulness
- **Practical Applications**: Directly addresses real-world problems (such as improving medical adherence, enhancing eyewitness testimony accuracy, or developing treatments for mental health disorders).
- **Theoretical Understanding**: Advances psychological knowledge by confirming, challenging, or revising explanatory models of human behaviour.
- **Provoking Further Research**: Highlights unanswered questions, prompts methodological refinements, or opens new sub-disciplines.
- **Methodological Validity**: High internal validity (controlling extraneous variables) and high ecological validity (reflecting authentic real-world settings).
- **Generalisability**: Diverse and representative sampling that allows insights to be applied across populations and cultural settings without cultural bias.

## Usefulness Across Areas and Perspectives

| Area / Perspective | Rating of Utility | Rationale and Core Study Evidence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Behaviourist** | High | Highly practical; demonstrates how reinforcement contingencies shape behaviour. **Chaney et al.** proved positive reinforcement via the Funhaler dramatically improved paediatric asthma compliance. |
| **Developmental** | High | Shows how childhood learning and environmental factors shape adult behaviour. **Bandura et al.** proved that children imitate aggressive models, transforming media guidelines and parenting strategies. |
| **Cognitive** | High | Directly informs educational revision and legal procedures. **Grant et al.** proved context-dependent memory improves academic performance; **Loftus & Palmer** demonstrated the fallibility of eyewitness testimony, reforming police questioning. |
| **Biological** | High | Provides physiological biomarkers and objective neural localisation. **Casey et al.** showed neural roots of impulse control in the prefrontal cortex and ventral striatum; **Maguire et al.** demonstrated hippocampal plasticity in navigation. |
| **Individual Differences** | Moderate to High | High clinical and diagnostic utility for neurodivergent or psychiatric groups. **Baron-Cohen et al.** developed the Eyes Test to identify theory of mind deficits in autism, though generalisability to the broader population is narrow. |
| **Psychodynamic** | Moderate | High therapeutic utility as the origin of modern psychoanalysis and talk therapy (**Freud**), but low scientific utility due to untestable, unfalsifiable concepts and unrepresentative case studies. |
| **Social** | High | Demonstrates how immediate situational pressures override individual dispositions. **Milgram** and **Bocchiaro et al.** explained atrocities, conformity, and disobedience, though historical or cultural variations affect temporal validity. |

## Practice Questions and Model Answers

### Q1) Explain how Grant et al.'s study into context-dependent memory can be considered useful (3 marks)
**Model Answer:** Grant et al.'s study into context-dependent memory is useful because it provides practical applications for students and educators. By demonstrating that participants who studied and recalled information in matching acoustic environments (silent/silent or noisy/noisy) achieved significantly higher test scores than those in mismatched conditions, the study provides evidence-based guidance for revision strategies. Students can enhance academic performance by revising in environments that replicate examination conditions.

### Q2) Outline the defining principles of the behaviourist perspective and discuss its usefulness as a perspective (4 marks)
**Model Answer:** The behaviourist perspective suggests that all behaviour is learned from interactions with the environment and focuses exclusively on observable behaviour rather than internal mental processes. Behaviour is shaped through conditioning, such as operant conditioning where positive reinforcement increases the likelihood of a behaviour recurring. This perspective is highly useful because it generates direct practical applications. For example, Chaney et al. demonstrated that using positive reinforcement with the Funhaler significantly increased medical compliance in children with asthma, proving that behaviourist techniques can improve healthcare adherence and health outcomes.
