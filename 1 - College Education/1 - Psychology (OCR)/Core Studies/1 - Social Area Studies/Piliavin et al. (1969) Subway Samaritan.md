## Piliavin et al. (1969) - Subway Samaritan

### 1. Theoretical Background
*   **Bystander Effect:** The phenomenon where individuals are less likely to offer help to a victim when other people are present.
*   **Diffusion of Responsibility:** The belief that in a large group, the sense of personal responsibility is spread out, making each individual feel less obligated to act.
*   **Cost-Reward Model:** Piliavin's theory that helping is a result of a subconscious calculation of the **costs** (e.g. effort, danger) and **rewards** (e.g. praise, reduced guilt) of helping versus not helping.

### 2. Aims
To investigate how the **type of victim** (ill vs drunk), **race of victim**, and **group size** affect helping behaviour in a naturalistic, unavoidable setting.

### 3. Methodology
*   **Method:** Field experiment.
*   **Design:** **Independent measures design**.
*   **Sample:** Approximately 4,450 passengers on the New York City Subway (Harlem to Bronx line). Estimated racial makeup: 55% White, 45% Black.
*   **Independent Variables (IV):**
    1.  **Type of Victim:** Ill (carrying a cane) vs Drunk (carrying a liquor bottle).
    2.  **Race of Victim:** Black or White male.
    3.  **Model Condition:** Early help (after 70s) vs Late help (after 150s) from a model.
*   **Dependent Variables (DV):**
    1.  Frequency of helping.
    2.  Speed of helping (latency).
    3.  Race and gender of the helper.
    4.  Qualitative verbal comments.

### 4. Procedure
1.  Four students (2 male actors, 2 female observers) boarded the train.
2.  The **victim** stood in the critical area and collapsed 70 seconds into the journey.
3.  In the **ill condition**, the victim carried a cane. In the **drunk condition**, he smelled of alcohol and carried a bottle in a brown bag.
4.  Observers recorded the time taken for help to arrive, the number of helpers, and the gender/race of those who assisted.
5.  If no help was offered, a **model** stepped in after either 70 or 150 seconds.

### 5. Results
*   **Quantitative:** The **ill victim** received spontaneous help in 95% of trials, while the **drunk victim** was helped in only 50%.
*   **Gender:** 90% of the first helpers were **male**.
*   **Race:** There was a slight "same-race" effect in the drunk condition (White people helped the White drunk victim more).
*   **Group Size:** No evidence of **diffusion of responsibility** was found; in fact, larger groups were often faster to help.

### 6. Conclusion
*   Victims who are perceived as "deserving" (ill) receive more help than those seen as responsible for their state (drunk).
*   In a confined space where escape is difficult, the **bystander effect** is less likely to occur.
*   Helping is a calculated response to the **arousal** caused by seeing someone in distress.

### 7. Detailed Evaluation & Improvements

| Feature | Evaluation & Details |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Research Method** | **Field Experiment**: High **ecological validity** as it took place in a real-life setting with unsuspecting participants. |
| **Design** | **Independent Measures**: Different passengers saw different conditions. This avoids **order effects** but can be influenced by participant variables. |
| **Sample** | **4,450 Passengers**: A very large and diverse sample, which increases the **generalisability** of the findings. |
| **Sampling Method** | **Opportunity Sampling**: Uses people who happened to be on the train. Efficient but may miss people who do not use the subway at those times. |
| **Data Type** | **Mixed Methods**: Collected **quantitative data** on help rates and **qualitative data** from passenger comments (e.g. "It's for men to help"). |
| **Ethics** | **Poor**: Participants were **deceived**, did not give **informed consent**, and could not withdraw. The situation may have caused **distress**. |
| **Weakness** | **Lack of Control**: Extraneous variables like train delays or the number of people in the carriage could not be perfectly controlled. |
| **Reliability Improvements** | **Standardisation**: The victims were identically dressed and followed the same script for collapsing, making the procedure **replicable**. |
| **Validity Improvements** | **Naturalistic Setting**: Because passengers did not know they were being studied, there were no **demand characteristics**, increasing **internal validity**. |

### Q1) Outline one way that Piliavin's study differed from previous laboratory research on helping (2 marks)
**Model Answer:** One difference is that Piliavin conducted a **field experiment** on a subway, whereas previous research by Darley and Latané took place in controlled labs. This meant Piliavin's study had higher **ecological validity** because it observed spontaneous helping in a real-world environment.

---

### Q2) Explain how the "Cost-Reward Model" can be used to explain the results of this study (3 marks)
**Model Answer:** The **Cost-Reward Model** suggests people help to reduce the **arousal** of seeing someone suffer. Helping the ill victim has high rewards (social approval) and low costs (little risk), leading to high help rates. Conversely, helping the drunk victim has higher costs (potential embarrassment or danger), so help is less frequent.

---

### Q3) Describe one finding from the study related to the race of the victim (3 marks)
**Model Answer:** The study found a slight **same-race effect**, but only in the **drunk condition**. White passengers were more likely to help a White drunk victim than a Black drunk victim. In the **ill condition**, however, there was no significant difference, as both Black and White victims received help almost universally.
