## Rosenhan (1973) - On Being Sane in Insane Places

### 1. Theoretical Background
*   **Medical Model:** The traditional approach in psychiatry viewing mental disorders as physical "illnesses" with biological causes, requiring medical diagnosis and hospital treatment.

*   **Diagnostic Validity vs Reliability:** **Validity** refers to whether a psychiatric diagnosis accurately reflects a real, distinct disorder. **Reliability** refers to whether different psychiatrists arrive at the same diagnosis for the same symptoms (consistency across diagnostic manuals like the **[[DSM-5]]** or **[[ICD-11]]**).

*   **Labeling Theory:** The sociopathological theory that once an individual is labeled with a psychiatric condition (e.g. "**[[Schizophrenia]]**"), all subsequent normal behaviours are misinterpreted through the lens of that psychiatric label (stickiness of psychodiagnostic labels).

*   **Institutionalisation & Depersonalisation:** The process by which psychiatric patients lose their individual identity, rights, and autonomy due to rigid hospital routines and isolation from staff.

---

### 2. Aims
1. To test whether **psychiatric diagnosis is valid** by observing whether sane individuals (pseudo-patients) attempting admission to psychiatric hospitals would be recognized as sane.

2. To investigate the **qualitative experience** of hospital life, staff-patient interactions, and the impact of institutional environment on patient treatment.

---

### 3. Methodology
*   **Method:** **Field experiment** and **participant observation** (Study 1); **naturalistic experiment** / rating scale survey (Study 2).

*   **Design:** **Independent measures design** (observations across multiple distinct hospital environments).
*   **Sample (Study 1):** Staff and patients in **12 psychiatric hospitals** across **5 US states** (East and West coasts). Hospitals varied in size, funding (state vs private), age (old vs new), and staff ratio.
*   **Pseudo-patients:** **8 sane individuals** (3 women, 5 men), including Rosenhan himself, three psychologists, a pediatrician, a psychiatrist, a painter, and a housewife. Used fake names and occupations (to protect career identities).

*   **Independent Variable (IV):** The fake symptoms presented by pseudo-patients upon admission.
*   **Dependent Variable (DV):** Admission decision, diagnosis assigned, length of stay, and staff treatment of pseudo-patients.

---

### 4. Procedure

#### Study 1: Main Pseudo-Patient Study
1.  **Gaining Admission:** Pseudo-patients called for an appointment complaining of hearing an unfamiliar voice of the same sex saying the words **"empty," "hollow," and "thud."** These symptoms were chosen because they lacked clear links to psychiatric literature.

2.  **Immediate Normal Behaviour:** Upon admission to the psychiatric ward, pseudo-patients **immediately ceased simulating all symptoms**, acted completely normally, and answered all personal history questions truthfully (except name and career).

3.  **Seeking Discharge:** Pseudo-patients had to convince hospital staff of their sanity through normal behavior to secure release.

4.  **Data Collection:** Pseudo-patients covertly took extensive field notes regarding ward life, staff behavior, and patient interactions. (When they realized staff ignored them, note-taking was done overtly in public spaces).

#### Study 2: Secondary Follow-Up Study
1.  **Hospital Challenge:** A research and teaching hospital claimed that such diagnostic errors could never occur at their institution.

2.  **Notification:** Hospital staff were informed that over the next 3 months, **one or more pseudo-patients** would attempt to gain admission.

3.  **Rating Scale:** Every staff member who interacted with incoming admissions was asked to rate each patient on a 1-to-10 scale regarding the likelihood of them being an impostor/pseudo-patient.

---

### 5. Results

#### Study 1 Results
*   **Admission & Diagnosis:** **100% of pseudo-patients were admitted.** 11 were diagnosed with **Schizophrenia**, and 1 with **Manic-Depressive Psychosis** (at a private, high-paying hospital).
*   **Length of Stay:** Ranged from **7 to 52 days**, with an average stay of **19 days**.
*   **Discharge Diagnosis:** All pseudo-patients were discharged with the diagnosis **"Schizophrenia in remission,"** proving that the diagnostic label was permanent and insanity was still assumed.
*   **Patient Detection:** While staff failed to identify the pseudo-patients, **35 out of 118 real patients** voiced suspicions (e.g., *"You're not crazy. You're a journalist or a professor checking up on the hospital!"*).
*   **Behavioral Misinterpretation:** Normal actions were viewed as symptoms of illness:
    *   Writing notes was recorded in medical charts as *"patient engages in writing behavior."*
    *   Arriving early for lunch was described as *"oral-acquisitive nature of the syndrome."*
    *   Pacing out of boredom was interpreted as *"nervous anxiety."*
*   **Powerlessness & Depersonalization:**
    *   Staff spent an average of only **11.3%** of their time outside the glassed nursing station.
    *   Psychiatrists spent an average of **0.4%** of their time directly interacting with patients.
    *   Doctors walked past **71%** of patient attempts to initiate eye contact/conversation without looking at them; nurses ignored **88%** of queries.
    *   **2,100 medication tablets** were administered to pseudo-patients (flushed down toilets).

#### Study 2 Results
*   Total patients evaluated over 3 months: **193 patients**.
*   **41 patients** were rated as pseudo-patients with high confidence by at least **one staff member**.
*   **23 patients** were suspected by at least **one psychiatrist**.
*   **19 patients** were suspected by **both** a psychiatrist and another staff member.
*   **Actual number of pseudo-patients sent by Rosenhan: ZERO (0)!**

---

### 6. Conclusions
*   **Invalidation of Psychiatric Diagnosis:** Psychiatric diagnosis lacks validity; medical professionals cannot reliably distinguish the sane from the insane in psychiatric institutions.
*   **Diagnostic Errors:** Psychiatric diagnosis is prone to **Type I errors** (false positives: diagnosing a healthy person as ill, as in Study 1) and **Type II errors** (false negatives: diagnosing a sick person as healthy, as in Study 2).
*   **Context Over Behavior:** Diagnostic labels dictate how behavior is interpreted; once a person is labeled "schizophrenic," the environment ensures all actions are viewed as pathological.
*   **Institutional Depersonalization:** Hospital environments cause severe depersonalization, powerlessness, and loss of dignity for patients.

---

### 7. Detailed Evaluation & Debates

| Feature / Debate | Evaluation & Analysis |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Research Method** | **Field Experiment & Participant Observation**: High **ecological validity** because it took place in real operational hospitals. Naturalistic observation allowed authentic insight into patient treatment. |
| **Generaliability** | **High Hospital Diversity**: 12 hospitals across 5 US states (old/new, public/private, rich/poor). However, sample of 8 pseudo-patients is small, and findings are **ethnocentric** to 1970s US healthcare. |
| **Reliability** | **Standardized Symptom Script**: High consistency across pseudo-patients reporting the exact same 3 words ("empty, hollow, thud"). However, field note observations were unstructured and prone to **observer bias**. |
| **Validity** | **High External Validity**: Real hospital settings meant staff acted naturally without **demand characteristics**. However, critics argue doctors operate on **trust** (expecting patients not to lie about auditory hallucinations), lowering construct validity. |
| **Ethics** | **Very Poor Ethical Standards**: Staff were **deceived** and could not provide **informed consent**. Pseudo-patients experienced stress and risk of harm. Valuable hospital resources were diverted from genuine patients. |
| **Individual vs Situational** | Strongly supports **Situational Explanations**: The psychiatric institution and label dictated how people were treated, rather than their actual sane individual traits. |
| **Reductionism vs Holism** | Highlights the **Reductionism** of the medical model, which reduces complex human social behavior to a single medical diagnostic label. |
| **Ethnocentrism** | **Ethnocentric**: Conducted entirely within the US mental health system in the early 1970s; may not reflect modern international psychiatric practices (e.g. NHS or ICD-11 criteria). |
| **Practical Application** | **High Reform Impact**: Spurred massive revisions of diagnostic criteria (leading to DSM-III), reduced long-term institutionalization, and promoted outpatient community mental health care. |

### Q1) Outline the symptoms presented by the pseudo-patients in Rosenhan's (1973) study to gain admission. (2 marks)
**Model Answer:** The pseudo-patients claimed they were hearing an unfamiliar voice of the same sex as themselves repeating the single words **"empty," "hollow," and "thud."** These specific symptoms were selected because they had no pre-existing link to psychiatric literature for schizophrenia.

---

### Q2) Explain what Rosenhan's study demonstrated about Type I and Type II errors in psychiatric diagnosis. (4 marks)
**Model Answer:** In **Study 1**, psychiatrists committed a **Type I error** (a false positive) by diagnosing 8 sane pseudo-patients as mentally ill and admitting them to hospital wards. In **Study 2**, when hospital staff were warned that fake patients would be sent, staff committed **Type II errors** (false negatives) by identifying 41 genuine patients as impostors when no pseudo-patients were actually sent. This demonstrated that psychiatric diagnostic decisions are heavily influenced by expectation and context rather than objective biological markers, rendering diagnosis invalid.

---

### Q3) Discuss the ethical issues raised by Rosenhan's study "On Being Sane in Insane Places." (5 marks)
**Model Answer:** A major ethical issue in Rosenhan's study was the **deception** of hospital staff, who were unaware they were taking part in research and were therefore unable to give **informed consent**. This breached institutional trust and potentially diverted medical time, care, and medication away from genuine psychiatric patients who needed care. Furthermore, the 8 pseudo-patients experienced **psychological distress** and powerlessness while hospitalized, with stays ranging up to 52 days, placing them at risk of psychological harm. However, Rosenhan argued these ethical violations were justified by the immense public benefit of exposing the invalidity and depersonalizing nature of psychiatric hospitalization.

---

### Q4) Compare the findings of Study 1 and Study 2 in Rosenhan (1973). (4 marks)
**Model Answer:** In Study 1, staff demonstrated a strong tendency to diagnose sane people as insane (**false positive / Type I error**), admitting 100% of the pseudo-patients into 12 hospitals. Conversely, in Study 2, when staff were primed to expect fake patients, they demonstrated the opposite tendency (**false negative / Type II error**), suspecting 41 real patients of being impostors when zero pseudo-patients were sent. Together, both studies prove that psychiatric diagnostic decision-making is unreliable and driven by environmental context rather than patient sanity.

### Q5) Assess the reliability of findings (6 Marks)
![[Pasted image 20260928091804.png]]

The findings regarding hospital staff show high internal reliability due to the use of a standardized procedure across trials. The pseudopatients were trained to approach staff using identical, scripted requests (such as politely asking about ground privileges) delivered in a consistent manner. Furthermore, the psychiatric hospital data is based on a large sample size of 1,283 attempted interactions across 12 different hospitals. This large volume of trials reduces the impact of anomalous behavior and demonstrates high internal consistency for the 88% figure recorded for staff moving on with head averted.

However, the reliability of the comparison group is significantly lower. The university control condition involved only 14 attempted interactions, which is a very small sample size that is vulnerable to individual participant variables and chance. Consequently, the 100% "Stops and talks" result lacks consistency and is difficult to reliably replicate. Additionally, inter-rater reliability is a concern across the study because pseudopatients recorded staff behaviors from memory after interactions occurred. Without a secondary independent observer to cross-check the coding of responses, such as distinguishing between "makes eye contact" and "pauses and chats", the data carries potential for subjective recording bias.

Overall, while the hospital findings demonstrate strong reliability through standardised scripting and a large number of trials, the overall findings are limited by the small control sample and a lack of inter-rater consistency checks
